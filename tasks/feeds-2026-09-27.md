## [Comment is Freed (Sam Freedman)] What happens if the Democrats sweep the midterms?
- url: https://samf.substack.com/p/what-happens-if-the-democrats-sweep
- date: Sun, 27 Sep 2026 08:51:38 GMT

Back in March, when I first looked at the midterms , the Democrats were already favourites to win the House. The Senate race was in the balance. But the downwards trajectory of presidential approval ratings and the poor quality of many Republican candidates led me to think it was winnable too.
 We are now five weeks out and a "blue wave" seems likely. The Democrats are eight points ahead in national polls and have a lead in key Senate races like Texas and Ohio. Some real longshots have moved from highly implausible to conceivable. There have even been a couple of polls showing them ahead in Kansas , a state which has not returned a Democratic senator since 1932. Election analysts like Nate Silver and G. Elliot Morris think the chances of a major Democratic win are being underestimated in Washington.
 It is not hard to understand why the Republicans' position is deteriorating. Trump's personal ratings have continued to fall, as I thought they would. His disastrous war in Iran has pushed up prices, and his antics are getting ever more absurd and distant from the interests of Americans. No political consultant would recommend choosing now to focus on renaming AI or a grotesque new ballroom in the White House. As this Economist chart shows, he is well below his first-term lows, or Biden's lowest point.
 


 
 Yet there has been surprisingly little commentary on what it would mean for American, and global, politics if the Democrats do take both chambers on November 3 rd . This is partly because Republicans dare not suggest Trump might be fallible, and liberals do not want to tempt fate. This is an understandable instinct. We have seen fairly substantial polling errors in the past, and there is a residual fear that a desperate Trump will do anything to try to stop a Democratic victory. But as I explained in another post in March , there is not a lot he can do in practice, beyond attempting a coup. The states run elections, not the federal government.
 In the rest of the post I will give an overview of where things stand in the key races. Then I'll look at what a blue wave would mean in practice. What could the Democrats do with congressional power? What would that mean for the war in Iran? What happens if we get a full-blown war between the executive and legislative branches? And what would it all mean for politics in other countries?

## [Mind of Things] Anduril and the Limits of Build First, Buy Later
- url: https://justinmc.substack.com/p/anduril-and-the-limits-of-build-first
- date: Sun, 27 Sep 2026 10:00:38 GMT

This week, Anduril announced that they were no longer able to work at risk on the FQ-44. A while back I wrote There is a Defense Tech Bubble , and this is one of the clearest examples those inside the bubble are starting to realize the limits of private capital. Private capital can finance prototypes, facilities, and even some low level production at risk, but it is not a indefinite substitute for Pentagon appropriations. The reasons Anduril had to make this call are myriad. There is a lot of strain in the market that is now starting to come to a head. A bubble does not mean fraud, it means capital and valuations are growing faster than the Pentagon's ability to convert tech into large, recurring procurement. Anduril has realized that too. The question is not whether Anduril's initial shift away from its "build first, buy later" philosophy is a signal to the Pentagon, it clearly is. The question is: Is anyone listening?

 Build First, Buy Later One of the largest differences between commercial and military technology is that, normally, the military starts with procurement. They buy first, then the company builds. This is a major criticism of the defense primes. They will not take on risks, and they expect their customer, the U.S. Government, to carry more risk than customers normally do. That's true. I carried no risk when Apple developed the Mac Mini or the new iPhone. The difference here is that I am one of 1.5 billion Apple customers. Defense primes have one major customer: Uncle Sugar. Sure, they have smaller customers in other governments, but the main driver is the Pentagon. So there are already significant risks when you work in defense tech: You are at the mercy of a monopsony.
 Combating the power of this monopsony was one of the only positive outcomes of "the Last Supper." Prior to the Clinton Administration, there were 51 defense primes in the U.S.; by the mid-2000s, there were five. This consolidation was natural, as it helped restrict supply and competition and allowed the primes to operate more freely. VC-backed neo-primes challenged this, increased competition, and allowed the U.S. government to start exercising more of its monopsony power.
 Anduril helped lead the neo-prime assault on the primes' old ways. Much like Palantir, they deployed engineers, built systems based on observed needs, then took those systems to the Pentagon as prototype solutions. Their actions helped add vigor to the rising chorus of "big primes bad," and they contributed to a growing belief that the Pentagon would soon be able to simply buy future weapons off the shelf.
 Except there is no shelf. There is no weapons-of-war store akin to Costco or Walmart where government procurement officers can walk up and down the aisles and pick out what they need. Part of the reason for this is that the Pentagon is not just a customer. It is most directly responsible for designing future warfare and building the plans for contingency operations. Unlike Henry Ford's likely apocryphal statement, "If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said a faster horse," the Pentagon shot all its horses. They are not always right, but what they need becomes the requirements they use to tell the primes what to build. The emergence of the neo-primes did not change the need to fill those requirements.
 A marketplace, of what In 2025, Ukraine launched the Brave1 marketplace . They expanded it to include custom drones in 2026. I think this is a great idea and works well for where Ukraine is now. The U.S. could do something similar. It could establish a contract vehicle that allowed unit level customers to buy qualified devices. 
 The issue is one of scale. Ukraine is using drones and all manner of military equipment at a rate that the U.S., even with the Iran War, is not approaching. A marketplace for drones in the U.S. military right now, even if units were empowered, would not drive meaningful revenues to sustain these defense tech companines. But, small USVs and electronic warfare systems are different than the FQ-44.
