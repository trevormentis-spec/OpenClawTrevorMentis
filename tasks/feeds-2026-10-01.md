The best employment advice I've ever heard is to choose your predecessors wisely. Andy Burnham's speech on Tuesday was well constructed and delivered with poise and emotion; it would have got good reviews regardless. But the rapturous reception in the hall and in much of the press owed a lot to the contrast with poor Keir Starmer's tortured performances.
 Back in June, when Starmer stepped down, I said I found it odd that so many people thought Burnham would be the same but with a northern accent and a TikTok account. It is even more surprising that some people are still saying it. The two men face many of the same problems, of course, but their approaches are very different. Burnham's priorities were already clear when I wrote that June post, and they were there again in his speech: social care, electoral reform, devolution, social housing, technical education and public control of utilities. Even after two years in power, it was impossible to know if Starmer had any. You might disagree with Burnham's beliefs, but they are consistent and genuinely held.
 The importance of this is evident to anyone who speaks to Downing Street advisers and officials, many of whom were held over from Starmer. They talk of a PM who is interested in what they are doing, wants to discuss policy and find the best way to convince the public of an argument. This is a complete transformation from the first half of the year. None of this should be particularly worthy of note; it should be the minimum requirement for a prime minister, but it wasn't there and now it is. Without priorities or purpose, any government will just get buffeted around by events. Having some allows you to keep returning to the core mission when things get difficult. And it allows ministers to make independent decisions that broadly fit an overall narrative.
 But having a general sense of where you want to go is different from being sure of the exact destination or knowing how to get there. To make it work, you need a map.
 There's a reasonably clear route to some of Burnham's objectives, especially those without fiscal implications. Proportional representation can be debated, stuck in the next manifesto and legislated for quickly if he wins. Much can be done on devolution over the next three years, and there has already been a big shift within government. When it comes to changing the economy, though, or transforming public services, there is not much he can do now, and little clarity over what will happen by when. A highly constrained budget to be followed by an even more painful spending review remains the defining reality of this parliament, and much of Tuesday's speech was an implicit acknowledgement that change in many areas is a long way off.
 Then there is a broader question about what this all means in practice. What do all the different themes he keeps coming back to, especially the vaguer ones like reindustrialisation and "a new economy", mean? The framing of the speech, again unchanged since this summer, is that we have been going in the wrong direction for the last forty years. But how much is he really intending to change? Is this a genuine reversal from "neoliberalism"? What would that even look like?

 NEW HAVEN&#8212;Long on ceremony but short on substance, the eagerly anticipated summit between US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping was a disappointment. Perhaps not a major disappointment, as expectations were generally low and as some talk is certainly better than no talk. But on the major issues of trade, AI, Taiwan, and addressing China's role in two theaters of war, there was little or no progress. Instead, ceremonial flourishes substituted for substance, unmasking the summit as yet another Trump vanity project.
 On trade, the two-month extension of the current truce&#8212;initially negotiated nearly a year ago in South Korea&#8212;was surprisingly short, implying that tariff uncertainty remains high. Pre-summit chatter hinted at an extension of 6&#8211;12 months. Moreover, there were expectations of another round of Chinese purchases of US agricultural exports. Instead, the two nations made a joint post-summit announcement to cut tariffs on a total of $60 billion of "non-sensitive" goods, including trivial products such as US imports of electric blankets and magic tricks and Chinese imports of eels and emus.
 On AI, the biggest issue facing civilization, there was no meeting of the minds. The two superpowers committed to a loosely structured dialogue and dangled the possibility of an unspecified " hotline " for AI-related national-security issues. This bare-minimum achievement is far from the major breakthrough on AI safety many had hoped for&#8212;especially in the aftermath of the recent Hugging Face hacking incident and reports of other intrusions that have led some AI insiders to raise human extinction concerns. The disappointing outcome of the summit &#8212; which merely paid lip service to such risks &#8212; is hardly surprising considering that Trump has doubled down on his hands-off approach to AI regulation.
 On Taiwan, Xi attempted to press the advantage he gained after the Beijing summit in May, when Trump described the pending $14 billion US arms package to Taiwan as a " good negotiating chip ." In view of America's normally staunch commitment to arming Taiwan, the Chinese side saw this as a major concession, especially given that the deal already had received congressional approval. In his opening speech to last week's summit, Xi urged the US to "adhere to the correct position of opposing Taiwan independence." But doing so would be a stunning about-face on the "strategic ambiguity" that has long shaped US strategy toward Taiwanese sovereignty.
 Finally, China's role in the wars in Ukraine and Iran escaped scrutiny. As the buyer of some 90% of Iran's oil exports and about 50% of Russia's, China is a major funder of both countries' military efforts. A new law gives the US president broad authority to address this activity through secondary sanctions and tariffs on those countries like China, that indirectly support these illegal wars. Don't count on it. Fearful of spoiling the summit's optics, Trump made no mention of this political hot potato.
 By making little effort on the big issues, both in terms of staff preparation and bilateral talks, Trump was painfully transparent about what mattered most to him: impressing Xi and his entourage. Ironically, while the tightly controlled Chinese press delivered enthusiastic messaging back home of Xi's great successes at the summit, the US broadcast media was muted by Trump's White House ban on three major news outlets, a shocking crackdown on free speech.
