 Second Thoughts on Open Source? : Is Beijing reconsidering the open-sourcing of China's most powerful AI models? Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Axios : "I think that they didn't realize how powerful their open-source models are."

That's one American official's interpretation. But there are increasingly concrete signs of concern within China's own AI governance apparatus and developer ecosystem. 

Take, for example, the AI Safety Governance Framework 3.0 , published in September by China's cybersecurity standards committee, known as TC260. "Once the autonomous attack capabilities of large foundation models spread downstream through open applications or open-source releases, they may significantly lower the barriers to conducting sophisticated cyberattacks, while accelerating the speed and expanding the scope of attacks," the framework notes. Over the summer, TC260 also invited organizations to help draft a national standard on the safety of open-sourcing AI models. 

Safety organization Concordia AI and model company Z.ai recently released a joint framework for open-weight AI risks. And the prominent scholar Zheng Yongnian (previously a vocal advocate of open source) now says : "We cannot simply say that closed models are good and open models bad, or vice versa. Openness and closedness are themselves relative concepts... If we ignore these boundaries and insist on unrestricted openness, managing the risks becomes a major problem."
 
 If a Chinese open-source model enables a major cyberattack, no one will be able to say the risks went unrecognized. Chinese developers are already confronting failures in their models' safeguards. The BBC reported that Moonshot was conducting an internal review after researchers bypassed its models' safeguards to obtain guidance on bioweapons and assassinations.

 So far, this is evidence of reassessment, not yet a reversal. Nothing in these examples indicates that Beijing has yet decided to stop the public release of its most capable models, and the TC260 framework continues to encourage open-source development. But this reassessment matters for American policy. We cannot assume China's current risk-tolerant posture toward open-weight models will endure. The question now is what safeguards Chinese authorities and developers will put in place to prevent these risks from becoming reality.

## [Conflict (Stephen Roach)] China and the AI Sweepstakes

Last week's release of the 2026 Global Innovation Index provides some important metrics to assess the Great Battle of our lifetime &#8212; the AI race between the United States and China. The GII providers a detailed assessment of some 80 key innovation drivers for 139 economies. I have written repeatedly about the GII for a number of years, extolling its virtues as the most comprehensive gauge of relative innovation potential across the world.
 Previously , I have underscored the dramatic narrowing of the innovation gap between the United States and China. This has, of course, been going on for a number of years, and not much has changed in 2026. China held on to 10 th place in the GII universe &#8212; about the same as its ranking in 2025 but up dramatically from 43 rd place in 2010. Over that same period, the United States moved from the 11 th position in 2010 to 3 rd place in 2026; since 2019, the US ranking has fluctuated in a tight range between 2 nd and 3 rd . Needless to say, China's ascendancy by 33 positions since 2010, dwarfs that of any other nation in the GII sample, including, of course, the United States.
 


 
 The GII is not designed to pinpoint the potential for any specific innovation. It covers a broad range of innovation inputs &#8212; institutions, human capital and research, infrastructure, market and business sophistication &#8212; that could go into any one of a number of innovative activities. The output side of the GII, which attempts to approximate that range of innovative activities, is equally broad; it stresses the "production" of innovative inputs into the all-encompassing categories of knowledge, technology, and creative outputs.
 What are we to make of such an abstract vision of a nation's innovative potential? My own suspicion is that the GII is best at capturing the rolling cycles of different innovations over time. In recent years, that could range from computer hardware and wireless telecommunications to alternative energy generation and electric vehicles. But now, of course, it seems reasonable to conclude that the GII currently bears most critically on the greatest innovation of our time &#8212; Artificial Intelligence.
 Of all the 54 detailed subcomponents on the input side of the Global Innovations Index, I would argue that test scores of high-school student matter most in assessing a nation's AI potential. Sure, financing capacity, data-based infrastructure, venture capital cultures, university-centric technology clusters, and regulatory oversight &#8212; just to name a few of the other prominent GII components that are related to AI development &#8212; all matter a great deal. But at the end of the day, secondary schools that produce super-smart hard-driving young entrepreneurs focused on the theory and applications of the deep science of artificial intelligence could well matter the most.
 On that count, there is a compelling case to argue that China is far better placed than the United States. This shows up in the so-called PISA scores of 15-year-old students, the most comprehensive comparative assessment of aptitude in reading, mathematics and science. Conducted every three years under the auspices of the Program for International Student Assessment , PISA scores have been a key component of the GII since its inception in 2007. As the chart below shows, with the exception of the three years from 2017-19, Chinese students have ranked number one in the world on PISA scores since 2011; by contrast, 15-year old high-school students in the United States have ranked in 24 th place, on average, over this same period. Putting it another way, the raw scores in the PISA examination for Chinese students were 15% higher than they were in the United States over the 2011-26 interval; in the latest PISA cycle (centered on 2025), that gap stood at 18%.

It is coordinated by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), and since 2000 has been conducted every three years (with a one-year delay in 2022 due to COVID-19). Starting in 2025, PISA has moved to a four-year testing cycle.
