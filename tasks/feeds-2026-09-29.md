## [Epoch AI] AI is getting cheaper faster than any other transformative technology
- url: https://epochai.substack.com/p/how-fast-is-ai-getting-cheaper
- date: Mon, 28 Sep 2026 15:33:44 GMT

This is a summary of a longer report on our website. 
 
 Would you be surprised if the sticker price on a new car fell from $50,000 to $296 in two years?
 Because that's how fast AI is getting cheaper.
 Over the past five years, the price of thought &#8212; the cost for an AI to hit a particular benchmark score &#8212; has fallen 13&#215; per year. It seems to be a faster pace than any other transformative technology has matched: it's four times faster than DNA sequencing, six times faster than compute, 18 times faster than lithium batteries, and (in the century up to 1973) 54 times faster than electricity. (OK, we don't have good data on the price of the wheel right after it was invented.)
 


 
 We measured the cheapest way to hit benchmark scores over time To investigate this trend, we calculated the cheapest way of hitting a given score on five AI performance benchmarks covering mathematics, hard sciences, and games of skill (e.g., chess) at different points in time over the last three years.
 For example, on January 31, 2025, OpenAI released a new iteration in its series of "reasoning" models, called o3. We estimate that for an average cost of 30 cents per question, it could achieve a 75% score on GPQA Diamond , a multiple-choice exam covering PhD-level physics, chemistry, and biology. Just under 18 months later, OpenAI released GPT-5.6 Luna. It scored just as well &#8212; for four hundredths of a penny per question ($0.0004). That is a 725-fold drop in the price of thought in under 18 months, equivalent to the sticker price on a new car falling from $50,000 to $69 in only a year and a half.
 This pattern of plunging price holds across benchmarks of math and chess skills too:
 


 
 Coarser evidence suggests that this rate goes back to the dawn of commercial LLM inference in November 2021, when OpenAI fully released GPT-3.
 That said, benchmarks are an imperfect proxy for what AI actually costs in the market or how that price compares to other technologies. Overall, we believe that our numbers are reasonably representative of reality, but they should not be read as exact.
 Prices fall 13&#215; per year; fastest soon after they release We find that the price for a given level of performance has fallen about 47% per quarter, or 13&#215; per year. We see slower drops on game-based puzzles, at about 39&#8211;43% per quarter (7&#8211;10&#215; per year), and faster progress on math problems, at 50&#8211;52% per quarter (16&#8211;19&#215; per year).
 The cost decline for each given level of performance tends to slow down over time. Cost falls 66% per quarter (75&#215; per year) at first. Two years later, it falls half as fast, at a "mere" 32% per quarter (4.7&#215; per year). One possible explanation: when a performance level is first achieved, AI companies can briefly charge a premium for it, before competition and technological improvement quickly drive down the price. In time, that dynamic slows.
 The cost of AI has been plunging for the past five years at rates unmatched by any of our reference technologies. Whether this trend continues &#8212; and what it means for the industry &#8212; are questions that only the coming years can answer.

The recently released Jev AI model has been quite a cultural phenomenon in technical communities in the past 2 weeks.
 While Jev aims to classify things, it's easy to dismiss Jev as "just a classifier," and my own view of Jev has evolved quite a bit over the past few days. In particular, my thoughts went from "classifiers used to be my bread & butter; I can easily build this myself" (more on this later) to "wow, this actually works better than I thought."
 


 Figure 1: Quick overview of the Jev API; more details on that later. 
 
 Sure, the latest state-of-the-art GPT and open-weight LLMs can do the same kinds of classification tasks as Jev, while also being capable of much more general decision-making. But Jev's advantage is that it can handle those classification tasks much faster and more cheaply.
 At the other end of the spectrum, for a narrow, well-defined problem, Jev probably won't classify anything better, faster, or cheaper than a special-purpose classifier. But its selling point is that it is far more general than those task-specific models.
 So, what is the methodology behind Jev (based on an educated guess), what can it do, and why is it so popular? I aim to answer all of these later in this article. However, I thought starting with a brief history of language models for decision-making would be a great way to begin. And it hopefully helps demystify some of the hype and show what Jev does very well ("Jev is essentially a text classifier," but "Jev is also not &#8216;just' a text classifier.")
 PS: I am not affiliated with Jev in any way. Also, I am not offered free access to Jev, and this is also not a product endorsement, just a technical article to offer some insights into the history of text classification to help you make sense of the recent hype. 
 Since this is a long article, I recommend reading it in your browser , where you can access the table of contents menu on the left side.
 1. Language modeling and classification in the pre-transformer era For completeness, before we put Jev in context (no pun intended), I thought it made the most sense to start chronologically. In this section, I want to take a brief tour of applied text classification via naive Bayes, logistic regression, and the more classic (deep) neural networks before transformer-based models came along.
 1.1 Bag-of-words: naive Bayes, logistic regression, and XGBoost Back in the day, when I was a grad student 15 years ago, even though recurrent neural networks already existed (more on that later), text classification was usually done with a bag-of-words representation because it was straightforward and could get good results on moderately sized datasets.
 In short, we can think of the bag-of-words representation as a method that makes free-form text input of different lengths compatible with classic classifiers (naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, SVMs, Random Forest, XGBoost, to name a few), which expect a fixed-size input vector.
 Popular real-world applications include anything from news article classification to email spam filtering. And yes, allegedly even Gmail's original spam filter used a Naive Bayes model with a bag-of-words representation.

Here I am announcing the launch of my Sunday school. The first, autumn semester will be running every week since October 10-11 till late December.
 As I have written previously, the first course will be centred around reading and discussing the Roman Revolution, by Ronald Syme.
 Imagine Muay Thai bootcamp somewhere in the jungles of Southeast Asia. In a camp, you would be building tolerance to the muscular pain. Now this course will be training your tolerance to the cognitive & epistemological pain.
 You don't know much, you are constantly facing the limits of your understanding, you feel puzzled and bewildered, and that is okay. In fact, that is great, because all of that builds your cognitive muscles.
 Reading this particular book will take some effort. This is one reason I have chosen it. You must read difficult books, if you want to assume control over your focus of attention. Focusing on a hard, demanding text is the opposite of doomscrolling.
 That, however, is not all. There are many difficult-to-read books around. Most of them, let's be honest, are not particularly rewarding. The fact that a book is difficult and painful to read, does not necessarily mean there is some great wisdom hidden behind it.
 Syme is different. In fact, the whole thing with Syme is that there is a great wisdom behind his writings. A rare and a particularly precious one. 
 What you discover reading Syme is the whole system of political physics, political mechanics . Deep and astute observations, on how things work, generally speaking. All given in a very dense, "packed" form, often aphoristically.
 What we will be doing, is unpacking it. Every lesson will start with a lecture, followed by the reading discussion. (The reading, I assume, you do on your own. Otherwise, there's not much point, because the muscles won't be building). 
 One thing I want to do in particular, is connect what we learn about Rome, and the ancient Mediterranean in general with other fields and spheres of knowledge. With other areas, regions, epochs. For a great deal of patterns, and mechanisms that were present in Rome, have been present not only in Rome, but far beyond its borders, and long after the Rome was gone.
 What we will aim to do, is to see the general behind the particular.
 For the history, as Lord Bolingbroke pointed out is nothing but philosophy that teaches by examples. And that is, perhaps, the best way to learn philosophy: that is the general patterns of how things work, under the moon.

## [Comment is Freed (Sam Freedman)] The Sudan War: is there any way out?
- url: https://samf.substack.com/p/the-sudan-war-is-there-any-way-out
- date: Tue, 29 Sep 2026 07:42:30 GMT

Since April 2023 Sudan has been consumed by a vicious war that gets far too little attention in the West. I am guilty of this. I described it in February 2024 as a &#8216;forgotten war' and have only returned to it once since. One reason for the neglect can be explained by the demands made by the other big wars in Europe and the Middle East. That is my excuse. Another reason is the difficulty facing casual observers in trying to understand where this particular war comes from and why it continues. 
 What people do know is that Sudan is a humanitarian disaster on a scale that is hard to grasp. The exact number of deaths is impossible to know. A count from last April put the number at 59,000 but this is undoubtedly an underestimate, with the actual number two or three times higher or more. Over 11 million people have been displaced, of whom around 4 million have sought refuge outside the country. According to the World Food Programme , nearly 19.5 million &#8203;people face high levels of acute food insecurity, of whom some 5 million face emergency or catastrophic hunger levels. Where battles rage people get trapped and aid cannot get to them. Farms have been destroyed and markets have stopped functioning. The warring parties often fail to allow humanitarian aid to get through as they see desperate conditions for civilians as a form of pressure on their opponents. 
 The WFP's ability to get relief to these people has been further weakened by the cuts to aid by rich countries. The Iran War has made its own contribution with rising diesel prices and fertilizer shortages adding to the challenges facing farmers. Poor sanitation, a lack of vaccinations, and a badly damaged health system all contribute to the spread of deadly diseases.
 The desperate need to get relief to those facing starvation and disease means that the war is viewed largely as a humanitarian catastrophe, as another African tragedy. But the tragedy has causes and these are political. The politics of Sudan at one level looks simple: a power struggle between the leaders of the national army and a very powerful militia. But look below and numerous other actors come into view, many smaller militias with their parochial interests, sometimes reflecting ethnic and religious identities but often largely economic. Then look beyond and regional powers loom large, funding the war and providing equipment, even as they discuss a possible peace.
 So while the suffering caused by the conflict argues for a quick peace, its underlying complexity makes one harder to achieve. Compromises will always be elusive with wars, and especially civil wars, that have been underway for some time, without either side close to a decisive victory. They stir up deep emotions, of fear and hatred, triumph and solidarity. Blustery rhetoric demonises the enemy so that any concessions can easily be portrayed as a sellout and shameful. Continuing war may mean more pain for little gain but any deal reached through compromise may leave a sense of lingering dissatisfaction and unfinished business - creating the conditions for an eventual resumption of hostilities.
 It often takes a third party - an international organisation or one or more major powers - to broker an agreement, but they can have their own interests in the outcome or else do not always stay around or do enough to ensure that any agreement is enforced. Peace-making may be a noble enterprise but it can frustrate all concerned. The deal-maker sees the benefits of peace lost because of foolish pride or unwarranted optimism about future victories. The belligerents see far too much trust being placed in the unreliable word of their enemies. When deals are made the results are often disappointing. Agreements can fall apart quite quickly so that fighting resumes. Even if they hold, in the aftermath of war life will still often be wretched and post-war reconstruction slow.
 This is not an argument for not trying. When the alternative is continuing with the horrors of war then one does not want to be fatalistic about reaching agreements or dismissive of their potential value. Even an unsatisfactory peace will be an improvement on continuing bloodshed and offers opportunities for creating conditions that can give it some permanence. But it is an argument for realism and against taking up peace negotiations with exaggerated expectations. The challenge is always to shape a lasting peace that will not break down the first time it is tested.
