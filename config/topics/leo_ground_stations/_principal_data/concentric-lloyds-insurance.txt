LLOYD'S OF LONDON · LEO GROUND-STATION INSURANCE

Who is actually insuring ground stations for LEO
networks out of Lloyd's
Bottom line up front. There is no single Lloyd's syndicate that writes a stand-alone “LEO ground-station” policy. Ground stations are
insured at Lloyd's through a stack of four parallel risk classes — space third-party liability, terrestrial property/all-risks, cyber, and
political violence/terrorism — placed by specialist space brokers across overlapping but distinct syndicates. The space-class
consortia (Brit-led Llift Space, Atrium 609, Hiscox MGA, Beazley 623/2623, Munich Re Syndicate 457, Tokio Marine Kiln, Chaucer,
Canopius) provide the satellite-side cover; the ground-asset perils sit with property, cyber, and PV/terror syndicates in the same
buildings.1

4. Market dynamics specific to LEO ground stations
■ Capacity is tight and shrinking. Lloyd's space-market capacity has not returned to 2018 levels; the 2023 loss year produced the
worst space-line result in 20+ years and pushed several insurers out. A handful of start-up space syndicates were lining up to
begin underwriting in late 2025 — the first material new entrants in years.4
■ LEO operators self-insure the satellites — but not the ground. By Gallagher's count, ~99% of LEO satellites are uninsured; LEO
constellations rely on in-orbit redundancy. That economics is inverted on the ground: a single gateway loss is not redundant in
the same way, so ground-station property, cyber, and PV are where the actual buy is for LEO networks. Underwriters see the
ground-segment as the more buyable, higher-frequency exposure.5
■ Cyber is the live concern. The 24-Feb-2022 KA-SAT attack — ground-network intrusion via a misconfigured VPN appliance,
lateral movement to a management segment, modem-wiper deployment — remains the canonical LEO/MEO ground-segment
cyber loss scenario in London. It is the reason Lloyd's pushed through the LMA5567A/B state-backed cyber war exclusions
effective March 2023, which now sit on every cyber slip touching satellite ground infrastructure.6
■ Space-segment cyber is the manuscript product to ask for. Coverage that “integrates the satellite, its ground infrastructure,
and the communication links as a single unified system” with system-restoration funds and BI for malicious-outage scenarios is
the form most relevant to a LEO gateway operator. It is written by space-and-cyber crossovers at Hiscox, Brit, Beazley, Tokio
Marine Kiln, and Munich Re 457, with Marsh and Aon ISB as the principal placing brokers.7

■ PV/terror is location-driven. Demand spikes for gateway sites in higher-threat geographies (LATAM, MENA, sub-Saharan Africa,
Pacific island nodes). Hiscox, Chaucer, Beazley 623, Canopius, Talbot 1183, Tokio Marine Kiln, and Aegis 1225 are the typical
Lloyd's lines on the PV/terror stack for fixed satcom sites — not the space box.
■ Brokers, not syndicates, hold the relationship. Because no single Lloyd's vehicle writes the whole ground-station risk, the
broker is the integrator. The four firms that effectively control LEO ground-station placement at Lloyd's are Marsh, Aon ISB,
Gallagher, and WTW, with Price Forbes and McGill & Partners as specialist wholesalers.

5. Implications for Concentric / KESSLER SENTINEL
Three takeaways for the Lloyd's space-syndicate go-to-market that sits behind KESSLER SENTINEL and the LEO ground-station
physical-security line:
(i) The buyer of LEO ground-station risk intelligence at Lloyd's is the broker, not the syndicate. The decision to bind a multi-class
stack — and therefore the demand for site-level physical security, cyber posture, and threat-geography data — aggregates inside
Marsh, Aon ISB, Gallagher, and WTW. Engagement priority should follow that order, with the specialist wholesalers (Price Forbes,
McGill) as a faster route in for bespoke programmes.
(ii) The named-underwriter map is small enough to work directly. The Lloyd's ground-station-adjacent space community is
effectively a dozen named people: Brice (Brit), Lecointe / Biagi / Bienvenu (Hiscox MGA), Bensoussan (Beazley), the Atrium 609 lead,
the TMK 510 aerospace head, plus Ogden's MRS-GMS team. A targeted briefing programme — KESSLER SENTINEL outputs +
ground-station physical-security capability deck — can cover the core leads in a single London week.
(iii) Cyber + PV/terror are the higher-conviction wedges. Pure space-class business is soft, capacity-constrained, and dominated by
long incumbents. The growth wedge that touches LEO ground stations is in space-segment cyber and PV/terror for fixed gateway
sites — both of which sit in different Lloyd's boxes (Hiscox, Brit, Chaucer, Canopius, Talbot 1183, Aegis 1225, TMK 510, Munich Re 457)
and therefore extend Concentric's addressable underwriter list well beyond the “space-only” bench.
