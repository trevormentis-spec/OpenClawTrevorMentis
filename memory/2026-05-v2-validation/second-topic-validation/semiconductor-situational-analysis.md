**BLUF**
The semiconductor supply chain will enter the second half of 2026 in a state of profound disaggregation. It is *almost certain* that geopolitical bifurcation will deepen, fracturing once‑global flows of chips, equipment, and critical raw materials. AI‑driven demand will *almost certainly* maintain its stranglehold on leading‑edge capacity, with NVIDIA’s dominance persisting and supply constraints in advanced packaging and high‑bandwidth memory (HBM) acting as a persistent brake on growth. Meanwhile, capacity expansions in mature nodes will *likely* outstrip demand, creating a two‑tier market where advanced logic commands premium pricing while legacy chips face overcapacity and margin erosion. The cumulative effect is a supply chain that is more resilient against broad disruption but acutely fragile at its most vital chokepoints. For investors with a multi‑year horizon, the landscape is defined by concentrated value creation in the AI stack, growing structural imbalances, and a premium on sovereign‑backed capacity that may not yield expected efficiencies.

---

### 1. Geopolitical Bifurcation and Critical Mineral Chokepoints

The semiconductor ecosystem is cleaving into US‑aligned and China‑aligned blocs, with the weaponisation of supply chains now extending from cutting‑edge fabrication tools to the raw materials that underpin chip production. The US CHIPS and Science Act ($52bn, with ~60% already allocated by mid‑2024) and the EU Chips Act (€43bn) represent the largest coordinated effort since the 1980s to rebuild domestic manufacturing. By H2 2026, the first wave of these incentives will have translated into physical fabs, but their impact will remain limited. TSMC’s Arizona facility, delayed by workforce shortages and cultural misalignment, is now slated to begin 4nm production in 2026—at least two years behind its original schedule. Even at full initial capacity (approximately 20,000 wafers per month), it will represent less than 2% of TSMC’s global output, leaving the West heavily dependent on Taiwan for sub‑7nm chips. It is *almost certain* that no comparable advanced fab outside Taiwan and Korea will reach meaningful volume before 2028.

While the West subsidises construction, China is escalating its asymmetric retaliation. The expansion of export controls to include antimony—a critical mineral used in flame retardants for semiconductor packaging, as well as in alloys and certain dopant materials—is a calibrated move. China accounts for roughly 50–60% of mined antimony and over 80% of global processing capacity. Unlike rare earths, antimony has no immediate substitute in several packaging applications, particularly for the epoxy moulding compounds that encapsulate chips. It is *likely* that these restrictions will cause price spikes and spot shortages in the near term, but *there is a realistic possibility* that Western mining projects in Australia, Canada, and the US, combined with recycling initiatives, could partially offset the disruption within 18–24 months. The real danger lies in the pattern: Beijing has demonstrated a willingness to weaponise its dominance in critical minerals (gallium, germanium, graphite, and now antimony) whenever the US tightens equipment export controls. This tit‑for‑tat cycle *almost certainly* will intensify through 2026, embedding friction costs and forcing supply‑chain managers to hold larger buffer stocks of both raw materials and finished chips.

The EU Chips Act’s €43bn target of a 20% global market share by 2030 will, by H2 2026, appear increasingly aspirational. Key projects such as Intel’s Magdeburg mega‑fab have been delayed, and the TSMC/ESMC joint venture in Dresden is focused on 28nm automotive‑grade chips—important for resilience but irrelevant for AI‑era leading‑edge needs. In practice, the Act is accelerating mature‑node capacity that may compound the overcapacity problem described below. The net result is a supply chain that is simultaneously becoming more localised and less efficient: it is *likely* that the total cost of ownership for a chip produced in a US or EU fab will remain 30–50% higher than in Taiwan or Korea, creating a permanent premium for “trusted” supply and bifurcating the market along geopolitical lines.

### 2. AI‑Fuelled Demand Concentration: The NVIDIA Dominance

The global semiconductor market reached $680bn on 12% year‑on‑year growth, but the headline figure conceals an extreme lopsidedness: AI‑centric chips are the primary growth engine, and NVIDIA commands roughly 85% of the AI training market. This concentration is *highly likely* to persist through H2 2026, despite determined efforts by AMD, Intel, and custom ASIC designers (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium). NVIDIA’s lock‑in rests not merely on hardware performance but on the CUDA software ecosystem—a self‑reinforcing moat of libraries, developer mindshare, and optimised frameworks that competitors cannot easily replicate within a two‑year window. Even if rival hardware proves competitive in benchmarks, enterprise adoption cycles and the inertia of hyperscaler software stacks make a rapid market‑share shift *unlikely*.

The dominance of a single designer creates a cascading set of supply‑chain bottlenecks. NVIDIA’s H100 and B‑series chips are manufactured on TSMC’s most advanced 4nm/3nm nodes, but the true chokepoint is CoWoS (Chip‑on‑Wafer‑on‑Substrate) advanced packaging, which TSMC has been racing to expand. By H2 2026, it is *likely* that CoWoS capacity will have more than doubled relative to early 2024 levels, yet demand from hyperscalers is projected to outgrow even that expansion. Similarly, the HBM memory stacks that accompany each GPU remain dominated by SK Hynix (and to a lesser extent Samsung), with production constrained by 1a‑nm process yields. The supply‑demand imbalance for HBM will *almost certainly* persist, pushing prices higher and forcing chip‑on‑wafer allocation decisions that favour the largest customers. A realistic possibility exists that startups and second‑tier cloud providers will be partially crowded out of the latest AI silicon, concentrating computational power further among the tech giants and raising antitrust concerns in the EU and US.

This AI boom has second‑order effects on the broader semiconductor plain. Leading‑edge wafer starts are finite; when AI accounts for an ever‑larger slice, it squeezes capacity for advanced automotive, networking, and CPU chips. It is *likely* that by mid‑2026, we will see selective shortages in high‑end MCUs and networking ASICs, as foundries prioritise the highest‑margin AI orders. Conversely, if enterprise AI adoption disappoints and the current $200bn+ capex cycle moderates, the blow to semiconductor equipment and materials suppliers would be significant. However, it is *unlikely* that a hard correction materialises before 2027, given the multi‑year commitments already announced by Microsoft, Amazon, and Google. The most balanced assessment is that AI‑driven demand will continue to grow through H2 2026, but the rate of growth will determine whether supply constraints act as a programme brake or merely a pricing tailwind.

### 3. Manufacturing Capacity: Regional Ambitions vs. Reality

The third situational factor is the growing divergence between advanced‑node scarcity and mature‑node glut. The CHIPS Act and EU Chips Act have incentivised a wave of fab construction, but most of the new capacity targets trailing‑edge nodes (28nm and above) and compound semiconductors (SiC, GaN) for automotive and industrial applications. Meanwhile, China, largely shut out of sub‑14nm equipment, is doubling down on legacy and mature‑node expansion. By one estimate, Chinese foundries could add more than 25% to global 28nm+ capacity by 2026. Most of this output is destined for domestic consumption, but a significant fraction will inevitably spill onto global markets, particularly if China’s economy remains sluggish and domestic demand absorbs less than expected. It is *likely* that this will create a price‑destructive overcapacity in mature nodes by H2 2026, pressuring margins at second‑ and third‑tier foundries such as GlobalFoundries, UMC, and SMIC (outside China). The risk is most acute for chips used in commodity consumer electronics, basic microcontrollers, and analogue semiconductors.

In contrast, sub‑7nm capacity remains tethered almost exclusively to TSMC’s fabs in Taiwan and Samsung’s in Korea, with Intel’s 18A process still a wildcard. TSMC’s overseas fabs—Arizona, Kumamoto, Dresden—are adding geopolitical resilience but not meaningful leading‑edge volume. The Arizona fab’s delay to 2026 illustrates a deeper truth: replicating the Taiwanese semiconductor ecosystem is a generational project. Workforce productivity, supplier co‑location, water and power infrastructure, and cultural differences in shift‑work practices have all contributed to slower ramp‑ups than in Taiwan. It is *almost certain* that, by H2 2026, no non‑Asian fab will have delivered a single wafer of sub‑5nm silicon. This means the industry’s most critical chokepoint—the Taiwan Strait—remains a singular point of failure. For a family office assessing tail risks, it is *virtually certain* that any serious disruption across the Strait would destroy more semiconductor value in a week than the CHIPS Act could rebuild in a decade.

The equipment landscape mirrors these dynamics. Leading‑edge lithography (ASML’s high‑NA EUV) and etch/deposition tools remain almost entirely sourced from a handful of Western and Japanese suppliers. Export controls have become more stringent, and it is *likely* that the US will further restrict Dutch and Japanese tool exports to China by 2026, particularly for tools that can support advanced node development. China’s response will likely be to accelerate domestic equipment development, but it is *unlikely* to achieve self‑sufficiency in critical tools before the end of the decade. For the supply chain, this means the equipment capex cycle will be increasingly bifurcated: Western and allied nations will invest in cutting‑edge tools, while China stockpiles used and secondary‑market equipment for legacy expansion.

### Strategic Implications

The interplay of these three factors yields a set of structural shifts that will define the semiconductor supply chain through H2 2026 and beyond:

**A. Fragility of the AI Supply Stack.** The AI revolution rests on a vertically concentrated bottleneck: CUDA software → NVIDIA architecture → TSMC advanced logic → TSMC CoWoS → SK Hynix HBM. Each layer is either a monopoly or a tight oligopoly. While this concentration has driven stunning performance gains, it is *almost certain* to be a source of systemic fragility. A single earthquake in Taiwan, a yield excursion at a packaging plant, or an abrupt escalation of export controls could send ripple effects across global cloud infrastructure. By H2 2026, the supply chain will have grown no more redundant; instead, the bottlenecks will have shifted from wafers to packaging and memory, but the concentration risk will remain critically high. Investors and policymakers alike will increasingly price in this geotechnical premium, and it is *likely* that new insurance‑like instruments or government‑backed strategic stockpiles will emerge, though their effectiveness is unproven.

**B. Bifurcation and the Rise of Two Semiconductor Worlds.** The combination of CHIPS Act flows, EU subsidies, and Chinese self‑sufficiency drives means that the semiconductor supply chain is splitting into two largely self‑contained ecosystems with different architectures, standards, and cost structures. For the US‑aligned world, the cost of resilience is permanently elevated capex and opex, which will be passed through to chip buyers. In the Chinese world, the priority is volume and self‑reliance, even at lower yields. This division creates a *likely* scenario where certain semiconductor categories (e.g., AI accelerators, advanced CPUs) become effectively embargo‑proof and decoupled, while mature‑node chips become a deeply commoditised, price‑competitive battleground. The role of neutral hubs—Malaysia, Vietnam, India—will grow as they offer assembly, test, and packaging services that can serve both blocs, but even these nations will face pressure to choose sides.

**C. Margin Divergence and Sectoral Rotation.** The supply‑demand imbalance between advanced and mature nodes will drive a stark margin divergence. TSMC’s leading‑edge gross margins will *likely* remain above 55%, benefiting from pricing power constrained only by political pressure. In contrast, mature‑node foundries could see margins contract toward 20–25% as Chinese capacity floods the market. This will accelerate a sectoral rotation: capital and talent will flow toward advanced logic, HBM, and AI‑specific ASICs, while legacy chipmakers face consolidation or reinvention as specialty providers. For equipment suppliers, the shift is a mixed blessing: demand for EUV and advanced etch will remain robust, but a glut of mature tools could depress orders. It is *likely* that the semiconductor equipment market will see a sharper bifurcation between a small number of advanced‑node winners and a larger group of mature‑node laggards, mirroring the chipmakers themselves.

**D. Raw Materials as a Persistent Shaper of Risk.** Antimony is the latest, but not the last, mineral to be weaponised. It is *highly likely* that by H2 2026, we will see similar actions on materials such as tungsten, indium, or quartz, each of which has limited non‑Chinese supply. The supply chain response—substitution, recycling, stockpiling—will be slow and expensive. This will embed a persistent materials‑cost inflation for packaging and specialty chemicals, narrowing the bill‑of‑materials headroom for chipmakers and OSATs. It is *unlikely* that a full‑scale desynchronisation of critical mineral dependencies can be achieved before 2030, meaning this risk will remain a recurring theme.

**E. The Timing Mismatch of Sovereign Capacity.** By H2 2026, the initial euphoria of the CHIPS Act and EU Chips Act will have met the reality of construction physics. Delays and cost overruns are *almost certain* to spark political recriminations and calls for accountability. While the allocated funds will still flow, the question will shift from “How much can we build?” to “How much did we pay for this output and when can we turn it on?” For a family office, the implication is that the narrative of reshoring is likely to undervalue the deep‑rooted structural advantages of existing Asian clusters, which will remain dominant well into the next decade. The strategic posture should therefore be one that acknowledges the long‑term intent but remains sceptical of near‑term execution.

In sum, the semiconductor supply chain of H2 2026 will be a study in contradictions: record‑high revenue and AI‑fuelled optimism, yet also unprecedented fragmentation and concentration risk. The forces of geopolitics, AI demand, and capacity regionalisation are each powerful on their own; together, they are reshaping an industry that was once designed for borderless efficiency into one that must now balance economic logic against existential imperatives. The only near‑certainty is that the comfortable assumptions of the pre‑2020 era will not return.
