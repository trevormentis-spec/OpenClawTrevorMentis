## 1. Executive Summary

### Bottom Line (85% confidence)

Brazil enters H2 2026 at a fiscal inflection point that carries both material opportunity and material risk for EM-focused family offices. The probability-weighted central case points to a **gradual, incomplete, and politically contingent** fiscal consolidation over the 2026–2027 horizon — sufficient to avoid crisis, insufficient to restore investment-grade credibility.

**The central judgment (45% confidence):** Brazil's gross public debt (IMF methodology) will rise from 92.8% of GDP in 2025 to approximately 99.0% of GDP by end-2027, while the primary balance improves from a deficit of 0.5% of GDP (2026) to near zero or a small deficit (2027). The Selic will decline from 14.50% to approximately 11.25% by December 2027, conditional on a peaceful election outcome and no sustained commodity price shock. USD/BRL will trade in a 4.50–5.45 range, with central tendency around 5.07 by year-end 2027.

**The risk asymmetry is skewed to the downside** — our probability-weighted estimate places 40% weight on adverse scenarios (downside + tail) versus 15% on the upside. The 2026 election is the critical branching point: a Lula victory preserving the current fiscal framework opens a slow-creep consolidation path (55% probability of base or upside under Lula continuity), while an opposition victory creates near-term uncertainty but could unlock structural reform (mixed directional impact).

**The single most important number to watch:** the primary balance. The government targets a surplus of 0.25% of GDP for 2026 under the fiscal framework. Every 0.1 percentage point deviation from this target translates to approximately 0.7 percentage points of additional debt/GDP over a three-year horizon through interest capitalization. We judge (moderate confidence, 60%) that the 2026 primary target will be missed by 0.3–0.6 percentage points, producing an effective deficit of 0.4–0.6% of GDP.

**For family offices, four implications dominate:**

1. **Duration risk in BRL-denominated fixed income is real but priced.** The Selic forward curve already embeds the base-case easing path. The tail risk is that inflation proves sticky and the Selic remains above 13% through 2027 — a scenario that would crush BRL duration positions.
2. **Real-denominated assets offer a premium but require election hedges.** The Bovespa's commodity-heavy composition provides a partial natural hedge against fiscal stress, but financial sector exposure (21% of index) is directly vulnerable to Selic staying higher for longer.
3. **The currency carry trade remains attractive at 14.50% Selic, but the volatility premium is compressed.** USD/BRL 1-year historical vol at ~18% versus 12-month forward implied at ~15% suggests the options market is priced for the base case, not the tail.
4. **Sovereign credit exposure should be limited to short-duration, hard-currency instruments.** The BB/Ba1 rating is stable for now but vulnerable to a post-election downgrade if fiscal consolidation stalls.

## 3. Current Fiscal Position & Baseline

### 3.1 The Starting Point

Brazil enters H2 2026 with fiscal fundamentals that are deteriorating relative to EM peers in every dimension except one: the country remains a net external creditor with substantial international reserves ($342 billion as of Q1 2026, source: BCB [B1]).

**Gross Public Debt** (Source: IMF Fiscal Monitor 2026, April [A1]):

| Metric | 2023 | 2024 | 2025e | 2026f | 2027f | 2028f |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gross Debt (IMF meth.) % GDP | 85.6 | 89.2 | 92.8 | 96.5 | 99.0 | 101.2 |
| Gross Debt (Dom. meth.) % GDP | 74.3 | 79.2 | 82.0 | 84.8 | 87.0 | 89.0 |
| Primary Balance % GDP | 2.1 | -0.3 | -0.6 | -0.5 | -0.1 | +0.1 |
| Nominal Deficit % GDP | -4.5 | -6.3 | -7.2 | -7.7 | -7.0 | -6.5 |
| GDP Growth % (Real) | 3.2 | 3.4 | 2.3 | 1.6–1.9 | 1.8–2.3 | 2.0–2.5 |

**Key observation:** The gap between IMF and domestic debt methodologies (currently ~12 GDP points) is driven primarily by Central Bank holdings of Treasury securities. Under BCB methodology, gross debt was 79.2% of GDP in February 2026. Market participants should use IMF methodology for cross-country comparisons but domestic methodology for trend analysis, as domestic is more relevant for fiscal solvency calculations.

### 3.2 The Fiscal Framework: What's Actually Binding?

Brazil's current fiscal framework, approved in 2023, sets **primary surplus targets** with a ±0.25 percentage point tolerance band. The targets are:

| Year | Target | Effective (with tolerance) |
|---|---|---|
| 2025 | 0.0% | -0.25% to +0.25% |
| 2026 | +0.25% | 0.0% to +0.50% |
| 2027 | +0.50% | +0.25% to +0.75% |
| 2028 | +1.00% | +0.75% to +1.25% |

**Reality check:** The IMF now projects a primary deficit of 0.5% for 2026 and 0.1% for 2027 (April 2026 Fiscal Monitor [A1]). This means the 2026 target will be missed by approximately 0.75 percentage points — a substantial gap.

The framework includes **automatic correction mechanisms**: if the primary target is missed by more than 0.25pp, the following year's spending growth is capped at 50% of the real spending growth floor (0.6% real). These triggers are expected to bind in 2027, limiting real spending growth to 0.3% — a meaningful fiscal tightening that would represent the most aggressive consolidation since the Temer administration (2016–2018).

**Moderate confidence (55%)** that these automatic triggers will operate as designed. Risks: (1) political override via emergency decree, (2) creative accounting to shift court-ordered payments outside the framework, (3) one-off revenue measures that inflate the headline balance without improving the structural position.

### 3.3 Court-Ordered Debt (Precatórios)

The precatório issue is the most significant accounting risk in the fiscal picture. A Reuters report from April 15, 2026 [B2] indicates the government will count only 39% of court-ordered debt payments within the 2027 fiscal target. The remainder will be classified as outside the fiscal framework, effectively allowing the government to present a primary surplus target while running a deficit that includes these payments.

**Calibration:** High confidence (75%) that precatório accounting will understate the true fiscal deficit by 0.2–0.4% of GDP in both 2026 and 2027.

### 3.4 The R$143.7 Billion Question

On May 12, 2026, the government announced R$143.7 billion (~2.5% of GDP) in new electoral-year spending (source: Brazil Business Insights [C2]). This package was framed as an "accelerated investment and poverty reduction program" ahead of the October 4 election. It includes:

- R$45.6B expansion of income tax exemption threshold (raising the floor from R$2,524 to R$3,200/month, benefiting approximately 15 million workers)
- R$38.2B in infrastructure acceleration program (PAC) front-loading (highways, water infrastructure, urban mobility projects concentrated in swing states — Minas Gerais, Bahia, Pernambuco, Rio Grande do Sul)
- R$32.1B in social program expansion (Bolsa Família top-up of R$225/recipient, minimum wage adjustment from R$1,518 to R$1,725 — representing 13.6% real increase over two years)
- R$27.8B in sectoral subsidies (agriculture credit at subsidized rates of 8.5% vs. Selic 14.50%, industrial development fund for the Manaus Free Trade Zone)

**Political motivation:** The package is transparently electoral. Approval drove Lula's polling numbers from 34% (March) to 39% (May) in Datafolha tracking. Valor International reports that economists view the package as fiscally irresponsible but politically rational — Lula needs a first-round knockout to avoid the risks of a runoff against a consolidated opposition in a volatile global environment.

**Assessment:** This spending package is **not fully funded** within the fiscal framework. Estimated revenue offsets total approximately R$85B (partial tax normalization, PIS/COFINS base broadening, dividend taxation changes, and a new minimum tax on high-income individuals), leaving a net fiscal impulse of approximately R$58.7B (~1.0% of GDP). However, this estimate likely understates the true cost because: (a) the revenue measures are back-loaded to 2027, creating a 2026 cash-flow gap, (b) the income tax threshold change is permanent (recurring cost of ~R$65B/year from 2027 onward), and (c) the supplementary budget mechanism allows Congress to add amendments without offsetting.

**Calibration:** Low-moderate confidence (40%) that the total effective fiscal stimulus this election year, including off-budget items, will reach 1.0–1.5% of GDP — well above what the fiscal framework contemplates. The trigger for a higher-confidence assessment will be the June mid-year budget review, which will reveal how much of this spending is classified inside vs. outside the fiscal framework.

### 3.5 Historical Context: How Bad Is This, Really?

Brazil's fiscal position is weak by historical and comparative standards but not unprecedented. The debt-to-GDP ratio (domestic methodology) peaked at 88.3% in November 2022, declined to 73.1% by January 2024 (post-election fiscal consolidation + strong GDP growth denominator effect), and has since climbed back. The current trajectory mirrors the post-2014 deterioration that culminated in Brazil's 2015–2016 recession and the 2016 presidential impeachment.

**Key difference from 2015:** Brazil's external position is far stronger (reserves: $342B vs. $362B in 2015; external debt: 28% of GDP vs. 35% in 2015; trade surplus: $67B vs. $20B deficit). The BCB is operationally independent. The fiscal framework provides automatic triggers that did not exist in 2015.

**Key similarity:** Political instability ahead of a presidential election, a fragmented Congress, and a commodity price cycle that could turn against Brazil. The combination of political uncertainty + fiscal deterioration + external shock is the same recipe that produced the 2015–2016 crisis.

**Calibration:** Moderate confidence (55%) that the 2026–2027 fiscal trajectory, while concerning, does not replicate the 2015–2016 crisis dynamics. The external buffer and institutional framework (BCB autonomy, fiscal framework) provide material, though not sufficient, protection.

## 4. Scenario Framework: Four Paths for Brazil

We structure the outlook around two critical uncertainties: (1) **Fiscal consolidation trajectory** (stays on track vs. deteriorates), and (2) **Political/institutional stability** (orderly transition vs. disruptive). This 2×2 matrix generates four scenarios.

### Scenario Matrix

| | **Fiscal on track** | **Fiscal deteriorates** |
|---|---|---|
| **Political stability** | BASE (45%) | DOWNSIDE (30%) |
| **Political disruption** | UPSIDE (15%) | TAIL (10%) |

### 4.1 Base Scenario: Slow Grind Lower (45% confidence)

**Probability:** 45% (Sherman Kent: Low-Moderate range, central within band)

**Narrative:** Lula wins re-election (or an opposition candidate wins with a reform mandate), the fiscal framework holds with automatic triggers, and the Selic declines gradually. Growth remains below potential at 1.6–1.8% in 2026 and 1.8–2.0% in 2027.

**Key assumptions:**
- Primary deficit narrows from 0.5% (2026) to 0.1% (2027)
- Gross debt (IMF) rises to 99.0% of GDP by end-2027
- Selic ends 2027 at 11.25% (Focus Survey consensus [B1], confirmed by Bloomberg survey May 11, 2026 [B2])
- USD/BRL trades 4.50–5.10 with year-end 2027 at 5.07
- GDP growth: 1.7% in 2026, 2.0% in 2027
- Credit ratings: stable at BB/Ba1, no change
- Brazil risk premium (EMBI+ spread): 250–320 bps

**Bullish triggers:** Faster-than-expected disinflation allows BCB to cut more aggressively; post-election reform consensus emerges; EU-Mercosur ratification boosts trade.

**Bearish triggers:** Inflation proves sticky above 4.5%; commodity price shock from Iran conflict; fiscal overruns beyond projected.

**Most likely single-path outcome:** Selic declines to 13.25% by end-2026 and 11.25% by end-2027. Debt/GDP rises continuously but does not trigger a crisis. Markets accept the slow deterioration as a "muddle through" equilibrium, similar to Brazil 2012–2014. *(High confidence: 80%)*

### 4.2 Upside Scenario: Reform Breakthrough (15% confidence)

**Probability:** 15% (Sherman Kent: Very Low range)

**Narrative:** The post-election environment produces a reform-friendly coalition — either Lula with a strengthened pro-market mandate or an opposition candidate (Tarcísio de Freitas or Flavio Bolsonaro) who prioritizes fiscal consolidation. The fiscal framework is strengthened, mandatory spending growth is structurally contained, and privatization resumes.

**Key assumptions:**
- Primary balance reaches surplus of 0.3–0.5% by 2027
- Gross debt (IMF) stabilizes around 94–96% of GDP by 2027
- Selic ends 2027 at 10.00% or below (Capital Economics forecast [C2]: 11.25% end-2026, but below consensus for 2027)
- USD/BRL strengthens to 4.20–4.50 range
- GDP growth accelerates to 2.5–3.0% in 2027
- Rating upgrade to BB+/Ba1 within 12–18 months

**Trigger events to watch:**
1. Post-election reform package in the first 100 days
2. Constitutional amendment limiting mandatory spending growth
3. Privatization of at least one major state-controlled enterprise (Petrobras minority stake, Correios)
4. Reduction of directed credit from BNDES and Caixa Econômica Federal

**Counterarguments (Why this is only 15%):** The structural political obstacles to fiscal reform in Brazil are deeply embedded. Congress maintains a fragmented distribution of power across 23 parties in the Chamber of Deputies. The 2026 election is unlikely to produce the supermajority needed for constitutional amendments. Even under an opposition victory, the reform agenda faces a minimum 18-month legislative timeline. *(Source: Allianz Country Risk Report [C2], AS/COA poll tracker [C2])*

### 4.3 Downside Scenario: Fiscal Drift (30% confidence)

**Probability:** 30% (Sherman Kent: Low range)

**Narrative:** Lula wins re-election with a weakened mandate (second-round squeaker), and the fiscal framework is progressively weakened through supplementary budgets, off-balance-sheet spending, and accounting maneuvers. The primary deficit remains stuck around 0.5% of GDP. Interest costs consume an increasing share of revenue.

**Key assumptions:**
- Primary deficit persists at 0.4–0.6% through 2027
- Gross debt (IMF) reaches 100%+ by 2027 and 104%+ by 2028
- Selic stays above 12.50% through 2027 (Xinhua/BCB Focus [B1]: market raised 2026 Selic forecast to 13.25% as of May 18 [B1])
- USD/BRL weakens to 5.20–5.50 range
- GDP growth drops below 1.5% in 2027
- Negative rating watch / downgrade to BB-/Ba2 within 12 months
- EMBI+ spread widens to 350–450 bps

**Escalation mechanism:** A primary deficit of 0.5% of GDP, combined with a Selic at 13% and growth below 1.5%, produces a debt dynamics trap (the "snowball effect"). Warren Rena estimates [C2] that under "do nothing" assumptions, gross debt rises from 84.8% (domestic methodology, 2026) to 114.5% by 2035.

**Forced dissent note:** Some sell-side analysts (notably at Itaú BBA and Credit Suisse) assign a higher probability (35–40%) to this scenario based on historical patterns of Brazilian electoral cycles. Their argument: every presidential election since 1994 (except 1998 and 2022) has produced a fiscal expansion of 1.0–2.5% of GDP that was only partially reversed in the post-election year. The 2026 cycle fits this pattern. We judge this argument as valid but over-weighted, as the 2023 fiscal framework's automatic triggers represent a structural improvement over previous rules. *(Source: IMF Fiscal Monitor [A1], Valor International February 11, 2026 [C2])*

### 4.4 Tail Scenario: Fiscal Crisis (10% confidence)

**Probability:** 10% (Sherman Kent: Very Low range)

**Narrative:** A contested election outcome, combined with an external shock (Iran conflict escalation driving oil above $120/bbl) and deteriorating fiscal fundamentals, triggers a loss of market access. The BCB is forced to hike Selic back toward 15% while the BRL collapses.

**Key assumptions:**
- Primary deficit remains above 0.8% of GDP
- Gross debt (IMF) breaches 105% by late 2027
- Selic hikes back to 14.75–15.00%
- USD/BRL spikes above 6.00
- GDP contraction of 0.5–1.5% in 2027
- Multi-notch downgrade to B+/B1 or below
- EMBI+ spread exceeds 600 bps
- Intervention by IMF or emergency fiscal package required

**Trigger events:**
1. Contested election (both candidates claim victory, Supreme Court intervention)
2. Oil price sustained above $120/bbl (Iran closure of Strait of Hormuz)
3. Simultaneous external crisis (EM sell-off, US recession)
4. Failed bond auction or sharp reduction in foreign participation in BRL debt

**Why 10% is materially relevant:** While 10% is a low probability, the portfolio impact of this scenario would be severe. A 10% probability event with 30–50% portfolio drawdown concentrated in BRL assets dominates the risk budget for EM-exposed family offices. This is the scenario that demands explicit hedging, not just diversification.

### 5.2 Selected Deep Dives

**Banks & Financials (24% of Ibovespa):** This sector faces the most direct fiscal-transmission risk. Higher-for-longer Selic benefits NIM in the near term but raises credit risk (non-performing loans historically lag Selic changes by 6–9 months with ~0.15 correlation per 100bp). The sector trades at 6.5× P/E [source: Bloomberg consensus, Apr 2026] — a 20% discount to 5-year average — reflecting embedded fiscal risk premium.

**Petrobras (11% of Ibovespa):** The semi-sovereign nature of Petrobras makes it a partial hedge against fiscal stress scenarios. The company benefits from oil price increases (Iran conflict tailwind) and would be a privatization candidate under an opposition administration. However, Lula's fuel price intervention risk (pre-election price controls or subsidy regime) creates binary risk around the October election.

**Calibration:** High confidence (75%) that Petrobras will maintain fuel price parity through September 2026. Thereafter, the trajectory is election-outcome-dependent. Moderate confidence (55%) that a Lula II administration would reintroduce some below-market pricing for diesel by Q1 2027.

**Consumer Discretionary (9% of Ibovespa):** This is the highest-beta sector to fiscal outcomes. Consumer credit penetration is at historical highs (~53% of GDP), and real wage growth is already decelerating (from 4.2% YoY in Q1 2025 to 2.8% in Q1 2026). The combination of high household indebtedness (48.5% of income), elevated Selic, and softening labor market makes this sector the most vulnerable in any scenario below base.

**Calibration:** High confidence (80%) that consumer discretionary earnings will contract by 5–10% in 2026 relative to 2025, with further downside of 10–15% under the downside scenario.

The first round of the presidential election occurs on **October 4, 2026**, with a likely runoff on October 25. Current polling (as of May 2026) shows:

**First Round:** Lula 36–40%, Flavio Bolsonaro 20–25%, Tarcísio de Freitas 12–16%, Ciro Gomes 6–9%, others <5% each.
**Runoff:** Lula 46–50% vs. Flavio Bolsonaro 38–43% (depending on which opposition candidate advances).

Polymarket probabilities as of May 19, 2026 [B2]: Lula 42%, Flavio Bolsonaro 37.1%, Tarcísio 15%, Ciro 5%.

**Key uncertainty:** Whether Tarcísio de Freitas (São Paulo governor, Bolsonaro-aligned but viewed as more technocratic) emerges as the opposition candidate instead of Flavio Bolsonaro. A Tarcísio candidacy would shift the fiscal calculus materially — he has a credible record of fiscal discipline in São Paulo state and signals support for structural spending reforms.

**Calibration:** Low-moderate confidence (40%) that Tarcísio will be the opposition candidate (versus Flavio). If Tarcísio: increase upside scenario probability by +5pp. Source: AS/COA Poll Tracker [C2], Polymarket [B2].

### 6.2 Fiscal Implications by Electoral Outcome

**Lula re-election (55% probability base case, 65% including runoff):**
- Continuity of current fiscal framework with gradual weakening
- Social spending expansion maintained
- Minimum wage growth above inflation
- No meaningful privatization
- Modest tax base broadening (dividend taxation, PIS/COFINS reform)
- **Net fiscal impact:** Primary deficit improves by 0.5% in 5 years, not the 1.0%+ currently targeted *(IMF A1 / Valor International C2)*

**Opposition victory (45% probability):**
- Near-term uncertainty (transition period Nov 2026–Jan 2027)
- Medium-term: potential for spending cap reform, privatization, BNDES restructuring

### 6.3 Congress & Reform Capacity

The 2026 elections also renew the Chamber of Deputies (513 seats) and one-third of the Senate (27 of 81 seats). The current Congress is highly fragmented: the largest party (PT) holds only 12% of seats. Constitutional amendments require 3/5 supermajority (308 deputies, 49 senators) in two rounds.

**Assessment:** Low confidence (30%) that the next Congress will have the composition to pass constitutional fiscal reforms. The electoral system's proportional representation and 16-party threshold (fragmentação partidária) make supermajorities structurally scarce.

## 7. Monetary Policy Interaction: Selic vs. Fiscal Dominance

### 7.1 Current Monetary Stance

The Selic stands at **14.50%** as of the April 29, 2026 Copom meeting (a 25bp cut from 14.75%). Copom voted unanimously for this reduction (Reuters, April 29 [B2]).

The committee's forward guidance is cautious: the Copom statement emphasized that "the pace of future adjustments will depend on the evolution of inflation dynamics, particularly the services component and inflation expectations, as well as the fiscal trajectory and external conditions."

### 7.3 Fiscal Dominance Risk

The concept of "fiscal dominance" — where fiscal conditions override monetary policy independence — is the single most important analytical question for the Selic path.

**Our assessment:** Brazil is at elevated risk of fiscal dominance but has not crossed the threshold.

**Evidence for dominance risk:**
- Public debt near 100% of GDP (IMF)
- Real interest rate above 8% (Selic 14.50% minus inflation expectations 4–5%)
- Primary deficit projected through 2028
- Interest costs at 8.07% of GDP (R$1.037 trillion in 12m through February 2026)
- Short duration of domestic debt (~3.2 years) creates refinancing sensitivity

**Evidence against dominance:**
- BCB operational independence (legally protected Central Bank autonomy since 2021)
- Moderate dollarization of debt (only ~4% of public debt is foreign currency-denominated)
- Reserve buffer provides external cushion
- Fiscal framework automatic triggers, while weak, are non-zero

**Calibration:** Moderate confidence (55%) that fiscal dominance will NOT materialize as a binding constraint on the Selic through 2027. If it does (our downside/tail scenarios), the impact is severe — the Selic could remain at or above current levels through 2028, breaking the entire forward curve.

### 7.4 The Iran Conflict Wildcard

The Iran-United States military confrontation that escalated in April–May 2026 has introduced a significant external inflation risk for Brazil. Brent crude rose from $76/bbl (late March) to a peak of $94/bbl in early May before partially retreating to $82/bbl as ceasefire talks progressed. Brazil, as a net oil exporter, benefits from higher oil prices (positive terms of trade effect of roughly 0.3–0.5% of GDP per $10/bbl increase sustained for 12 months). However:

- **Inflation channel:** Higher fuel prices feed into transportation costs (8% of IPCA basket), food distribution costs, and industrial inputs. The BCB estimates that each $10/bbl sustained increase adds 0.25–0.35 percentage points to 12-month IPCA.
- **Monetary channel:** The BCB explicitly cited the Iran conflict and its impact on inflation expectations in its decision to cut by only 25bp (rather than 50bp) in April. Forward guidance emphasized that the pace of easing is conditional on oil price stability.
- **Balance of risks:** A prolonged Iran conflict (our tail event trigger) that pushes Brent above $120/bbl would force the BCB to pause the easing cycle entirely and potentially reverse it. This would shift our scenario probabilities by approximately +5pp to downside and +5pp to tail, compressing the base from 45% to 35%.

**Calibration:** Moderate confidence (55%) that the Iran-US conflict will de-escalate to a manageable level (Brent below $90/bbl) by Q4 2026, removing the oil price tail risk from the fiscal equation. This is based on Polymarket ceasefire probabilities (~65% likelihood of formal ceasefire by September 2026) and the Trump administration's incentive to avoid an oil price spike in an election year.

### 8.3 The EU-Mercosur Wildcard

The EU-Mercosur trade agreement, signed in January 2026 but referred to the EU Court of Justice for compatibility review, represents a structural positive for Brazil's external sector. Provisional application is expected to begin in 2026. Under full implementation:

- Beef exports to EU could rise by nearly 80% (Deloitte [C2])
- Machinery exports to EU could increase by 15%+
- Brazilian manufacturing output could contract 5%+ as domestic industries face competition
- Net trade effect: moderately positive (0.1–0.2% of GDP)

**Calibration:** Low-moderate confidence (40%) that the agreement achieves full ratification within 24 months. Provisional application is more certain (65% confidence within 12 months).

**Proposition:** Limit BRL duration to <2 years in H2 2026. Extend to 3–4 years only after the election outcome is clear and the fiscal direction is confirmed (approximately February–March 2027).

**Hard currency bonds:** Brazil's 2034 Eurobond trades at G-spread of ~280 bps, roughly fair for the BB rating. We see no compelling tactical opportunity in hard currency sovereign debt at current levels.

## 12. Recommendations: Portfolio Positioning & Risk Management

2. **Maintain but size-limit the carry trade.** The 14.50% Selic carry is attractive, but use the options market to hedge the election tail: buy 3-month USD/BRL calls at 5.50 strike (cost: ~R$0.12 per USD, or ~2.4% premium on notional). *(High confidence: 80%)*

3. **Overweight Petrobras as a fiscal-geopolitical hybrid hedge.** The combination of oil price tailwind (Iran conflict), semi-sovereign credit profile, and election-driven optionality (privatization premium under opposition) makes this the single best risk-adjusted position in Brazilian equities. *(High confidence: 75%)*

4. **Underweight consumer discretionary.** The combination of high household debt, decelerating real wages, and sticky inflation creates a negative earnings revision cycle. Avoid until at least Q2 2027. *(Moderate confidence: 65%)*

### 12.2 Strategic (2027 and beyond)

5. **Position for election outcome** (approximately $1M allocation per $100M EM portfolio):
   - Lula victory: accumulate short-duration BRL bonds, start building long positions in infrastructure-linked equities
   - Opposition victory: overweight BRL duration (extend to 4–5 years), accumulate bank stocks, go long on privatization proxies (energy, logistics, airports)

## 13. Forced Dissent / Minority Views

*This section presents arguments that challenge the central assessment. Inclusion is a methodological requirement, not an endorsement.*

### 13.1 "The Fiscal Framework Is an Illusion" — Minority View

**Argument:** Brazil's 2023 fiscal framework has no credible enforcement mechanism. The automatic spending triggers can be bypassed through: (1) supplementary credit authorization (Congress can override caps with absolute majority), (2) classification of spending as "exceptional" and outside the framework, (3) the revenue-side manipulation of the primary balance through one-offs. The framework's failure to prevent the R$143.7B electoral spending package already demonstrates its weakness.

**Response:** The counterargument is that the framework has already survived its first stress test (2024 miss was contained) and that the automatic triggers, while imperfect, are a significant improvement over the absence of any binding constraint from 2016–2023 (the "spending ceiling" was effectively dead from 2019). We rate the original assessment's reliance on the framework at moderate confidence.

### 13.2 "The BRL Is Structurally Overvalued" — Minority View

**Argument:** Brazil's real effective exchange rate (REER) has appreciated ~15% from its 2022 lows, driven by carry trade inflows rather than productivity improvements. The "commodity supercycle" narrative is exhausted. As China's economy slows and the iron ore price declines from $110/t to a sustainable $70–80/t, Brazil's terms of trade will deteriorate by 5–10%, requiring a REER depreciation of 10–15%. This would push USD/BRL to 5.50–6.00 regardless of domestic fiscal policy.

**Response:** This argument understates Brazil's diversification. Agricultural exports (soy, beef, sugar, coffee) have pricing tailwinds from structural factors (global protein demand, sugar-as-biofuel demand) that are independent of China. Additionally, oil production from the Santos Basin is ramping toward 3.5M bpd, with pre-salt production costs at $5–8/bbl — among the lowest globally. We rate this minority view as valid but overstating the magnitude of required depreciation. We incorporate a broader range on our USD/BRL forecasts.

### 13.3 "The Upside Scenario Is Priced Out — It Could Be Larger Than 15%" — Minority View

**Argument:** Markets are systematically pessimistic about Brazil's reform capacity. Every reform cycle since 1994 (Real Plan, Fiscal Responsibility Law, 2016 spending ceiling, 2023 fiscal framework) faced similar skepticism before being implemented. An opposition victory (particularly Tarcísio) could produce a reform cycle comparable to 2016 (Temer's PEC do Teto) but with stronger political capital.

**Response:** The material difference is that the 2016 reforms followed a presidential impeachment and acute crisis. The 2026 context is normal political competition with no crisis catalyst. Reforms without crisis are harder to pass in Brazil's fragmented Congress. We maintain 15% as the disciplined assessment but acknowledge the possibility of higher regime-change probability.
