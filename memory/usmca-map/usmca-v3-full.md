# Executive Summary

The July 1, 2026 USMCA joint review is not a renegotiation — but the political incentive structure on all three sides ensures it will function as one. Our base case (**likely, 55–65%**) is that the review concludes with the agreement extended for a further 16 years, but only after a bruising six-month negotiation cycle in which the Trump administration extracts concessions on Chinese transshipment, rules-of-origin tightening in autos and steel, and Mexican enforcement commitments on labor, fentanyl, and energy-sector treatment of US investors. We assess the probability of outright USMCA collapse or US withdrawal notice at **unlikely (10–15%)**, but the tail is fatter than consensus pricing implies given the administration's demonstrated willingness to use Section 232 and IEEPA authorities outside the agreement framework.

**The central analytical judgment**: Mexico enters this review structurally weaker than in 2018. The Sheinbaum government inherited a fiscal deficit near 5.9% of GDP (SHCP, 2024 Criterios Generales — **B2**), a constrained Pemex balance sheet, and a judicial reform that has materially raised the perceived sovereign and contract-risk premium for foreign investors. Mexican peso volatility since the June 2024 election (BIS, **A2**) and the roughly 18% MXN depreciation against USD from April 2024 highs through Q4 2025 (Banxico, **A1**) reflect this repricing. Sheinbaum's negotiating leverage is further compressed by Mexico's 83% export dependence on the US market (INEGI 2024, **A2**) — a structural asymmetry that Washington negotiators understand explicitly.

## Political Picture

The trilateral political alignment heading into the July 1, 2026 review is the most adversarial since NAFTA's 1993 ratification fight, but the asymmetry of domestic incentives matters more than the rhetoric. We assess that **all three governments have stronger incentives to extend than to terminate (highly likely, 80–90%)**, but the path to extension runs through a confrontation cycle that markets are currently underpricing.

**Sheinbaum's position — constrained but coherent.** President Claudia Sheinbaum enters the review with a Morena supermajority in both chambers (Cámara de Diputados: 364/500 seats; Senado: 83/128 — Source: INE certified results, June 2024; A2) and approval ratings holding at 68–72% through Q4 2025 (Source: El Financiero tracking poll, November 2025; B2). This gives her domestic room to absorb tactical concessions that López Obrador could not have stomached. Her negotiating posture has been notably more technocratic than her predecessor's — Economy Secretary Marcelo Ebrard retains the file, providing continuity with the 2018–2020 NAFTA-to-USMCA transition and a direct USTR relationship. **We judge it likely (60–70%) that Sheinbaum will accept materially tighter rules-of-origin and a formal Chinese-investment screening mechanism in exchange for a clean 16-year extension**, framing it domestically as defending the *T-MEC* rather than capitulating.

The constraint is the judicial reform overhang. The September 2024 constitutional amendment subjecting federal judges to popular election (Source: DOF publication, September 15, 2024; A1) remains the single largest US and Canadian grievance outside trade itself, with USTR's 2025 National Trade Estimate explicitly citing it as a Chapter 14 investor-protection concern (Source: USTR NTE 2025, p. 287; A1). Sheinbaum cannot reverse it. She can — and we expect she will (**likely, 55–65%**) — offer procedural carve-outs for USMCA-covered investment disputes, possibly via a bilateral side letter.

## Sector Exposure

The USMCA review's economic bite is highly concentrated. Five sectors account for roughly 71% of Mexico's $506bn in goods exports to the United States in 2024 (Banxico balance of payments, B-2): autos and auto parts (~$193bn), electronics and electrical machinery (~$87bn), agricultural products (~$45bn), medical devices (~$25bn), and steel/aluminum and downstream metals (~$22bn). Review risk is not distributed proportionally across this base — it is bunched in autos, steel, and agriculture, where domestic US political constituencies are organized and the Trump administration has already telegraphed grievances.

**Autos — the central battleground (highly likely to dominate negotiations, 85–95%).** The 75% regional value content (RVC) threshold and the 40–45% labor value content (LVC) rule are the most likely targets for tightening. USTR's 2025 USMCA Implementation Report (B-2) flagged "persistent under-compliance" in light vehicle RVC certifications, and Ford and Stellantis have both publicly endorsed raising the threshold to 80–85% to squeeze Chinese-content inputs. The exposed names for subscriber portfolios are the Mexican-assembled platforms with thin RVC margins: Nissan Aguascalientes, Audi San José Chiapa, BMW San Luis Potosí, and Stellantis Toluca. We assess it is **likely (60–70%)** that the review produces an RVC ratchet to 78–80% phased over 36 months, which would force $4–7bn in supply chain reconfiguration capex across the Mexican OEM footprint (Open Claw estimate from AMIA production data, C-3). Tier-2 and tier-3 parts suppliers with Chinese-origin steel, electronics, or rare-earth inputs face the steepest adjustment.

**Electronics and the nearshoring thesis (the most exposed to transshipment enforcement, probably 55–70%).** Mexico's electronics export surge — up 34% since 2022 (INEGI, B-2) — has been driven disproportionately by Chinese-owned or Chinese-supplied assemblers in Nuevo León, Chihuahua, and Jalisco. Foxconn, Lenovo, Hisense, and BYD's component operations are the visible names; the harder problem is the long tail of Chinese tier-2 suppliers servicing Mexican-flagged exporters. We judge it **likely (60–70%)** that the review introduces either origin-tracing requirements for electronics inputs or a "non-market economy" content cap modeled on the Section 301 framework. Nearshoring narratives that treat Mexican electronics FDI as monolithically bullish are mispricing this bifurcation: Taiwanese, Korean, and Japanese-anchored capacity is largely insulated; PRC-anchored capacity faces a real probability of stranded asset risk.

**Agriculture — politically loud, economically contained (likely a flashpoint, unlikely to drive headline risk, 55–65%).** The 2024 GM corn dispute panel ruling against Mexico (B-1) defused the largest single agricultural irritant, but tomatoes (the suspension agreement lapsed July 2025), avocados (USDA inspector security incidents), and sugar remain active. Family office exposure here is limited unless held through Mexican agribusiness equities (Bachoco, Gruma) where the read-through is indirect.

**Scenario 1 — Bruised Extension (Base Case): 55–65% (likely)**

The review concludes between July and December 2026 with all three parties agreeing to a 16-year extension, but only after a confrontation cycle that runs from roughly Q1 2026 through the formal July 1 anchor date and likely spills into Q3.

Mechanically: USTR publishes its review report in early Q2 2026 (statutory deadline March 2026 under the USMCA Implementation Act §611, A-1) framing Mexican non-compliance on Chinese transshipment, energy sector treatment of US investors, and labor rapid-response mechanism enforcement. Trump escalates publicly — we expect at least one tariff threat episode in the Section 232 or IEEPA frame — and Sheinbaum responds with calibrated concessions: tighter rules-of-origin verification for autos, a formal Chinese investment screening regime modeled loosely on CFIUS, and accelerated USMCA labor panel resolutions. Canada plays the stabilizer role, as it did in 2018–19.

The peso trades to 21.50–22.50 against the dollar through the confrontation window (vs. 18.40 spot, B-2), IPC underperforms EM by 8–12%, and auto OEM capex decisions on Mexican greenfield slip by two to four quarters. Resolution comes with a joint communiqué, side letters on transshipment and energy, and the extension clock resets to 2042.

This is our base case because **the cost-benefit calculus for all three parties favors extension once the political theater is priced in (likely, 60–70%)**, and because the 2018–19 NAFTA→USMCA transition is a directly analogous precedent (B-2). The 30–40% residual probability mass distributes asymmetrically to the downside.

---

**Scenario 2 — Annual Review Purgatory: 20–30% (roughly even chance, lower half)**

The review fails to produce trilateral consensus by July 1, 2026, triggering the USMCA Article 34.7 fallback: the agreement remains in force but transitions to annual joint reviews through 2036, with termination possible on any anniversary with six months' notice.

The trigger is most likely a Sheinbaum red line — we judge **energy sovereignty and the Pemex/CFE treatment most likely to be non-negotiable (likely, 55–70%)** given Morena's constitutional reforms and the political cost of being seen to concede to Trump on hydrocarbon sector opening. A secondary trigger path runs through Canadian intransigence on dairy supply management or digital services taxation, with Mexico caught in the crossfire.

Market consequences are materially worse than Scenario 1 because the annual review cycle institutionalizes uncertainty. Each subsequent July becomes a discrete event risk. We estimate FDI commitments to Mexico decline 25–40% on a trailing basis through 2027–28 (B-3, modeled), peso settles in a 22.50–24.00 range, and the equity risk premium on Mexico-exposed industrials widens 150–250bps. Critically, this scenario does **not** mean tariffs — USMCA preferences remain in force — but it functionally ends Mexico's nearshoring narrative as a one-way trade.

This scenario is underpriced. Polymarket and Kalshi do not currently offer clean contracts on the Article 34.7 transition specifically, but related "USMCA renewed by [date]" framings appear to embed roughly 10–15% probability on non-extension paths, which we view as **too low (likely, 60–70%)** given Sheinbaum's domestic constraints.

Mechanically, withdrawal requires six months' notice and Congressional action to take full effect, so the rupture window plays out across Q4 2026 through mid-2027.

**B. Sherman Kent Probability Bands**

Directional judgments use the Kent lexicon, calibrated as follows:

- Almost certain: 95–99%
- Highly likely: 80–94%
- Likely: 55–79%
- Roughly even: 45–55%
- Unlikely: 20–44%
- Highly unlikely: 5–19%
- Almost no chance: 1–4%

Where ranges are given (e.g., "likely, 55–65%"), the band reflects our internal disagreement, not the lexicon range.

**C. Glossary of Key Terms**

- **USMCA Article 34.7:** The joint review and 16-year sunset mechanism. Triggers July 1, 2026 (six years from entry into force on July 1, 2020).
- **10-year annual review track:** Procedural path if any party declines to extend at the joint review; parties meet annually for ten years to attempt resolution, after which the agreement terminates absent extension.
- **IMMEX / Maquila regime:** Mexican program allowing duty-free import of inputs for manufacture and re-export. Distinct from USMCA rules-of-origin but commercially intertwined.
- **Section 232 / Section 301:** U.S. statutory authorities (Trade Expansion Act 1962 §232 national security tariffs; Trade Act 1974 §301 unfair-practice tariffs) available outside USMCA.
- **RVC (Regional Value Content):** Threshold for tariff-free treatment under USMCA Chapter 4; currently 75% for passenger vehicles, with labor value content (LVC) overlay.
