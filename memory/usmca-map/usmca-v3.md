# Executive Summary

The July 1, 2026 USMCA "joint review" is not a renegotiation in the legal sense, but the political optionality embedded in Article 34.7 — any party may decline to extend, triggering a ten-year sunset countdown — makes it the single most consequential governance event for North American capital allocation between now and the 2028 U.S. election. For family offices with Mexico exposure, whether through industrial real estate, manufacturing equity, peso-denominated fixed income, or cross-border logistics, the review is best understood as a **structured negotiation under asymmetric leverage**, not a binary cliff. Our base case (Sherman Kent: **probable, 65–75%**) is that USMCA is extended with material side-letter concessions from Mexico on three vectors: Chinese transshipment/origin rules, energy sector access for U.S. investors, and labor enforcement under the Rapid Response Mechanism.

The downside tail is non-trivial. We assess a **roughly even chance (40–50%, "probable" lower band)** that the review concludes with a one-to-three-year conditional extension rather than the full sixteen-year renewal envelope, introducing rolling uncertainty that compresses Mexico's terminal multiple even if no tariffs are imposed. A hard non-renewal scenario — sunset clock activated — we rate **unlikely (10–15%)** but with convex consequences: peso to 22–24/USD, IMMEX manufacturing capex frozen, and a 150–250bp widening in Mexican sovereign spreads versus Treasuries.

Four developments since the November 2025 Mexican submission to USTR shape our positioning:

1. **Sheinbaum's tariff posture has hardened, not softened.** The 50% steel and 50% aluminum tariffs on non-FTA partners imposed in late 2025, and the proposed Chinese vehicle tariff schedule circulated to Mexican industry in Q1 2026, signal Mexico City is preemptively conceding on the China question to preserve auto rules-of-origin treatment. *(Source: Secretaría de Economía communiqués; Admiralty B2.)*
2. **The U.S. position is internally bifurcated.** USTR Greer's working-level posture favors extension with reforms; the Lighthizer-aligned faction in the White House Trade Council favors using non-renewal as leverage for a 2027 bilateral rewrite. We rate the latter faction's influence **even chance** of prevailing on specific sub-issues (rules of origin, dispute settlement), but **unlikely** to capture the headline decision.
3. **Canadian alignment is now a swing variable.** Carney's government has signaled willingness to accept a trilateral framework but is exploring bilateral fallbacks. This reduces, rather than increases, Mexico's negotiating leverage — a development underpriced in current peso forwards.
4. **Sectoral carve-outs are being pre-negotiated.** Autos, semiconductors, and agriculture appear headed for separate annexes, which insulates roughly 60% of bilateral trade flow from headline risk but concentrates tail risk in the remaining 40% (textiles, steel derivatives, electronics assembly, pharmaceuticals).

**Portfolio implications, summarized:** We recommend family offices maintain Mexico industrial real estate exposure but shift duration shorter (sub-5-year leases preferred), hedge MXN exposure on a 60–70% delta through December 2026, and treat any sub-19.00 peso print as a hedging opportunity rather than a directional signal. ## Political Picture

The political geometry of the 2026 review is defined by three principals whose incentive structures only partially overlap: Claudia Sheinbaum in Mexico City, Donald Trump in Washington, and Mark Carney in Ottawa. The triangulation is unstable, and the instability is the trade.

**Sheinbaum's domestic position is stronger than her external leverage.** Morena and allies hold a qualified majority in the Chamber of Deputies and a working majority in the Senate following the September 2024 constitutional reform package (Source: Cámara de Diputados, Admiralty A2). Her personal approval has tracked between 70-78% across major Mexican pollsters through Q3 2025 (Source: El Financiero / Mitofsky composite, Admiralty B2). This gives her unusual room to absorb a hostile review cycle without immediate domestic political cost — but it also removes the pressure valve that a weaker president would use to compromise early. We assess it **likely (60-75%)** that Sheinbaum enters the review window prioritizing sovereignty optics over deal-closure speed, particularly on energy, judicial reform, and the lithium nationalization framework — each of which Washington and Ottawa have already flagged as Chapter 14 and Chapter 22 concerns (Source: USTR 2025 National Trade Estimate, Admiralty A2).

The López Obrador-era judicial overhaul — direct election of judges, completed first cohort seated September 2025 — is the single largest structural irritant. USTR's written submissions to the House Ways and Means Committee in October 2025 explicitly named the judicial reform as creating "investor protection deficits inconsistent with USMCA Chapter 14 obligations" (Source: House W&M hearing transcript, Admiralty A1). Sheinbaum has no political capacity to roll this back; it is constitutionally entrenched and ideologically central to the Fourth Transformation project. We judge it **almost certain (90%+)** that judicial-reform-linked investor-state friction features as a headline U.S. grievance in the review, and **probable (55-70%)** that it becomes a formal sticking point rather than rhetorical noise.

**Trump's incentive structure cuts in two directions.** The administration's revealed preference through 2025 — IEEPA tariffs in February, the fentanyl-linked 25% action, the steel and auto Section 232 escalations — suggests a willingness to use trade instruments coercively even against treaty partners (Source: Federal Register notices, Admiralty A1). At the same time, the Detroit Three, the Business Roundtable, and the Farm Bureau have all submitted aggressive pro-extension comments to USTR's docket (Source: USTR docket USTR-2025-0011, Admiralty A2). The political reading: Trump wants concessions visible enough to claim a "renegotiated" win — likely on rules of origin, Chinese transshipment, and possibly a bilateral side-letter on energy — but does not want the capital-markets disruption of a formal non-extension. We assess **likely (60-70%)** that the U.S. extends conditionally, with public demands attached, rather than triggering the ten-year sunset clock.

Carney is the wildcard most underpriced by U.S.-focused analysts. His April 2025 election mandate was explicitly framed around Canadian economic sovereignty vis-à-vis Trump, and Ottawa has signaled openness to bilateral CUSMA arrangements that would leave Mexico exposed (Source: PMO statements, June 2025, Admiralty B2). The "two-track" risk — where Canada and the U.S. extend bilaterally while Mexico is forced into a separate, weaker arrangement — is the tail scenario family offices should price. We judge it **unlikely but not remote (15-25%)** that a formal bifurcation occurs by July 2026, but **probable (55%)** that the threat of bifurcation is used as negotiating leverage against Mexico City in Q1-Q2 2026.

## Sector Exposure

Sector-level vulnerability to the 2026 review is not uniform, and the family office reflex to treat "Mexico exposure" as a single factor risk is the most expensive error available in this cycle. We disaggregate into five buckets, ranked by our assessment of asymmetric downside between now and Q3 2026.

**Automotive and auto parts (highest exposure).** The sector accounts for roughly 25% of Mexican manufacturing GDP and ~32% of goods exports to the U.S. (INEGI, 2024 annual; **B2**). Rules-of-origin tightening is the single most likely binding outcome of the review — *highly likely* (80–90%) in Kent terms — because it is the one demand on which the USTR, the UAW, and Senate Finance Republicans converge. The 75% regional value content threshold and the 70% steel/aluminum sourcing rule are both on the table for upward revision, and the labor value content (40–45% at ≥USD 16/hr) is the most probable vector for tightening because it doubles as a wage-floor mechanism palatable to Democrats. Pure-play Mexican Tier-1s with Chinese JV content (Nemak's aluminum exposure is cleaner; Rassini and Metalsa less so) face the steepest re-rating risk. Light-vehicle assemblers with deep U.S. footprints (GM, Stellantis) can absorb; Asian OEMs with Mexico-only North American capacity (Nissan, Kia, BMW San Luis Potosí) cannot. We assess a *likely* (60–75%) probability that at least one Asian OEM announces a North American capacity shift or delay by Q2 2026.

**Steel, aluminum, and upstream metals (high exposure, already pricing).** Section 232 tariffs were reinstated in March 2025 at 25%/10% with no country exemptions, and the melt-and-pour provisions for USMCA-origin steel are already being interpreted restrictively by CBP (Federal Register notices, Mar–Jun 2025; **A2**). The review provides the legal vehicle to harden these into treaty text. Ternium and Altos Hornos-adjacent supply chains are the cleanest short expression; the long expression is U.S. mini-mills (NUE, STLD), though that trade is crowded.

**Electronics and appliances (moderate-to-high, bifurcated).** Mexico's $80B+ in electronics exports (Secretaría de Economía, 2024; **B2**) is split between legacy assembly (Foxconn, Flex in Guadalajara/Juárez) and the post-2022 nearshoring wave (Quanta, Inventec, Wistron). The bifurcation matters: legacy assembly is largely USMCA-compliant on origin but exposed to any new digital-trade or forced-labor provisions; the nearshoring wave is overwhelmingly Chinese-anchored and represents the single largest political target after autos. We judge it *probable* (55–70%) that the review produces either a China-content cap or a transshipment enforcement regime that materially raises compliance costs for this cohort. Industrial REITs with tenant concentration in this segment (Vesta, Fibra Prologis, Terrafina) warrant tenant-by-tenant decomposition rather than headline AUM analysis — a point we extend in Section 6.

**Agriculture and agri-food (moderate, asymmetric).** Mexico is the U.S.'s largest agricultural export market ($30B in 2024; USDA FAS; **A1**) and the dependency runs both directions — yellow corn, soy, pork, dairy. ## Economic Dynamics

## Economic Dynamics

The macro backdrop into which the 2026 review lands is materially weaker than the one that greeted USMCA's 2020 entry into force, and the asymmetry matters for how Mexico can absorb a bad outcome.

**Growth trajectory.** Banxico's latest survey of private forecasters (October 2025 release) puts 2025 real GDP growth at 0.6% and 2026 at 1.3%, both revised down from 1.2% and 1.8% respectively in the July round (Banxico *Encuesta sobre las Expectativas*, B-2). INEGI's IGAE monthly proxy printed -0.7% y/y in August 2025, the third negative print in four months (INEGI, A-1). We assess with **high confidence** that Mexico enters the review window in technical stagnation, and with **moderate confidence** that a shallow recession (two consecutive negative quarters) is the modal path through H1 2026 absent a fiscal impulse Sheinbaum has so far declined to deliver.

**Fiscal space is the binding constraint.** The 2026 *Paquete Económico* submitted September 8 targets a public sector borrowing requirement (RFSP) of 4.1% of GDP, down from an executed 5.7% in 2024 — the largest single-year consolidation since 2016 (SHCP *Criterios Generales*, A-2). Public debt (SHRFSP) is projected at 52.3% of GDP year-end 2026. The consolidation is real but it consumes the counter-cyclical buffer: there is no room for a Trump-shock stimulus without breaching Sheinbaum's own anchor and risking the sovereign rating. Moody's (Baa2, negative) and Fitch (BBB-, stable) have both flagged USMCA review risk explicitly in 2025 commentary (Moody's August 2025 credit opinion, B-2). We judge it **likely** (60-70%) that at least one agency moves to negative outlook within 90 days of a Trump signal that the U.S. will not extend.

**Currency and capital flows.** The peso has traded a 17.80–20.40 range against the dollar through 2025, with implied vol on 6-month USDMXN options sitting at 11.2% as of mid-November (Bloomberg composite, B-2) — roughly 250bp above the trailing five-year median and pricing a meaningful left tail into the review window. Foreign holdings of Mbonos have declined from 49% of outstanding in 2019 to 14.1% at September 2025 (Banxico custody data, A-1), which paradoxically reduces the fast-money exit risk but also signals that the marginal foreign buyer has already left. The carry remains structurally attractive (Banxico overnight at 7.50%, expected to reach 7.00% by Q2 2026 per the survey median), but carry is a poor hedge against a 2017-style Trump tariff headline; we saw 8–11% peso drawdowns on single tweets during that cycle.

**Trade and FDI flows are already softening.** Total Mexican exports to the U.S. grew 2.1% y/y in Jan–Sep 2025 versus 7.8% in the comparable 2024 period (U.S. Census, A-1). Announced greenfield FDI fell 23% y/y in H1 2025 (fDi Markets, B-3), and Banxico's balance-of-payments FDI series shows reinvested earnings carrying ~78% of the headline — meaning *new* capital commitments have effectively paused. We assess with **high confidence** that the nearshoring thesis as a flow story is on hold pending review clarity
