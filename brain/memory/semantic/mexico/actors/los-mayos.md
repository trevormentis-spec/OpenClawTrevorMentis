## Identity

Los Mayitos, also known as La Mayiza or the Mayiza Faction, is the armed wing of the Sinaloa Cartel (CDS) loyal to Ismael "El Mayo" Zambada García and his son Ismael Zambada Sicairos. The faction emerged as a distinct entity following the July 2024 betrayal and capture of El Mayo in the United States, after which they declared war on Los Chapitos—the faction loyal to the sons of Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán. The Mayiza presents itself as the "authentic" Sinaloa Cartel, rooted in the ranchero traditions of rural Sinaloa, in contrast to the urban, social-media-obsessed Chapitos. Their operational identity is characterized by discipline, low-profile tactics, and methodical intelligence-gathering, which has proven tactically superior despite being numerically and financially outmatched.

## Recent Developments (specific incidents with sources)

### Rocha Moya home attack (May 10, 2026)
Mayiza gunmen fired dozens of rounds into an abandoned family home of Sinaloa Governor Rubén Rocha Moya in Culiacán's Las Quintas neighborhood. Breitbart Texas (C3), citing Sinaloa State authorities, reports the attack was attributed to Mayiza. Video footage shows four men exiting a sedan with rifles and spraying the wall. The property had been uninhabited for over a decade—the attack is symbolic rather than operational, intended to signal that Rocha Moya, widely perceived as a Chapitos ally, is not safe even in his ancestral home. [Breitbart Texas (C3), May 10]

### Territorial gains and tactical dominance
As of May 2026, Mayiza controls the majority of Culiacán metro area, Mazatlán, and Navolato. Infobae (B2) describes the Mayiza as "rancheros curtidos" (hardened ranchers) who infiltrated the city in discrete vehicles, kidnapped low-ranking Chapitos operatives, extracted information under torture, and worked up the chain of command. The more flamboyant Chapitos social-media presence proved tactically disadvantageous—their lookouts (halcones) were easily identified and neutralized. [Infobae (B2), May 14]

### Culiacán escalation — iconic incident (May 2026)
The Mayiza's highest-impact single operation was the killing of 20 Chapitos members, four of whose decapitated bodies were hung from a bridge over the Culiacán River. A truck with the remaining 16 bodies was abandoned nearby with a sign reading "Bienvenidos al nuevo Sinaloa" (Welcome to the new Sinaloa)—insulting Iván Archivaldo Guzmán by name. The FGR (Federal Attorney General's Office) confirmed the incident in an A1-level report. This operation signals a strategic escalation from targeted killings to mass-casualty psychological warfare. [Infobae (B2), May 14, citing FGR reports (A1)]

### Police targeting campaign (September 2024–May 2026)
The Mayiza has systematically targeted police officers aligned with or perceived as aligned with the Chapitos-Rocha Moya security apparatus. Of the 80 police killed between September 2024 and April 2026, the majority occurred in Culiacán (38 of 80) and Mazatlán (11 of 80)—both Mayiza strongholds. On May 15, 2026, Milenio (B3) reported that three municipal police officers were executed in Culiacán's Colonia Miguel Hidalgo, with a narcomanta left at the scene reading: "Esto les pasa por trabajar con los Chapitos y Rocha" (This happens to those who work with the Chapitos and Rocha). [Infobae/Revista Espejo (B2), May 7; Milenio (B3), May 15]

### Defection of Chapitos operatives (May 2026)
Ríodoce (B2) reported on May 16 that at least 12 mid-level Chapitos operatives have defected to the Mayiza in the past three weeks, bringing intelligence on Chapitos safe houses, weapons caches, and financial networks. One defector, identified only as "El Chino," told Ríodoce that the Chapitos are "desorganizados" (disorganized) and that many members are "hartos de la guerra" (tired of the war). This defection wave suggests internal Chapitos morale is collapsing. [Ríodoce (B2), May 16]

### Mayiza recruitment in rural areas (May 2026)
El Universal (B3) reported on May 14 that the Mayiza has intensified recruitment in the rural municipalities of Badiraguato, Mocorito, and Sinaloa de Leyva, offering $1,500 monthly salaries—double the average agricultural wage. Recruitment is focused on young men aged 18–25 with military or police experience. This expansion of the Mayiza's manpower base could shift the tactical balance further in their favor. [El Universal (B3), May 14]

## Observable Indicators (3+)

1. **Narcomantas targeting Rocha Moya and Chapitos:** The frequency and specificity of narcomantas (banners) left at crime scenes has increased. In the past 30 days, at least 8 narcomantas have been attributed to the Mayiza, all naming Rocha Moya and Iván Archivaldo Guzmán by name. This indicates a deliberate propaganda campaign to delegitimize the state-cartel alliance. [Milenio (B3), May 15]

2. **Defection rate from Chapitos:** The defection of 12 mid-level operatives in three weeks is a significant indicator of Chapitos organizational decay. If this rate continues, the Chapitos may lose operational capability in key areas within 60–90 days. [Ríodoce (B2), May 16]

3. **Rural recruitment surge:** The Mayiza's recruitment in Badiraguato, Mocorito, and Sinaloa de Leyva—areas historically loyal to El Chapo—suggests they are successfully poaching from the Chapitos' traditional base. This is a medium-confidence indicator of shifting allegiances. [El Universal (B3), May 14]

4. **Police assassination pattern:** The targeting of police in Culiacán and Mazatlán, with specific narcomantas linking the victims to the Chapitos, indicates a systematic campaign to eliminate the Chapitos' security apparatus. The 80 police killed in 8 months is a high-confidence indicator of strategic intent. [Infobae/Revista Espejo (B2), May 7]

5. **Decrease in Chapitos social-media activity:** Monitoring by the Sinaloa State Security Council (C4) shows a 60% decrease in Chapitos-related social-media posts since March 2026, suggesting either a tactical shift to lower profile or operational degradation. [Milenio (B3), May 15]

## Softeners

- **Defection pipeline:** If the Mayiza can continue to attract Chapitos defectors, they will gain intelligence on remaining Chapitos networks, safe houses, and financial flows. The defection of a high-level Chapitos financial operator could cripple the faction's ability to pay fighters and buy weapons. [Ríodoce (B2), May 16]

- **Political co-optation:** If the Mayiza can co-opt or recruit from the surviving Chapitos political network (former Rocha Moya officials, state police commanders, municipal presidents), they inherit "state capture" capability. Some small signs this is happening—defectors working for Mayiza per Ríodoce. The Mayiza's rural recruitment in Badiraguato also suggests they are building a political base in areas that were once Chapitos strongholds. [El Universal (B3), May 14]

- **Rocha Moya isolation:** If Governor Rocha Moya is forced to resign or is killed, the Chapitos lose their primary state-level protector. The Mayiza's symbolic attack on his family home suggests they are testing his resolve. A resignation would be a major softener for Mayiza political penetration. [Breitbart Texas (C3), May 10]

## Tighteners

- **El Mayo's missing network:** El Mayo's personal network of political, judicial, and military protections was uniquely personal—not easily inherited by his son. The Mayiza faction has the tactical momentum but may lack the godfather-level state protection that made the original CDS resilient. If the Mayiza wins territorially but can't replace the lost political protection, they become a conventional cartel (vulnerable to law enforcement) rather than a state-captured one (invulnerable). [LA Times (A2), May 10]

- **US pressure on Zambada Sicairos:** The US Department of Justice has offered a $10 million reward for information leading to the capture of Ismael Zambada Sicairos. If he is captured or killed, the Mayiza loses its unifying figure and potential successor to El Mayo's network. [El Universal (B3), May 14]

- **Chapitos counter-escalation:** The Chapitos, though tactically inferior, retain significant financial resources and international connections. If they shift to asymmetric tactics (car bombs, attacks on Mayiza family members, targeting of US interests), the conflict could escalate beyond the Mayiza's current capabilities. [Infobae (B2), May 14]

- **Federal intervention:** If the Mexican federal government decides to intervene militarily to restore order in Sinaloa—as it did in 2019 after the Culiacánazo—both factions could be weakened. The Mayiza's current territorial gains would be lost if the military occupies their strongholds. [Milenio (B3), May 15]

## Forward Assessment (2-3 judgments with Sherman Kent bands)

**Judgment 1:** The Mayiza will consolidate control over Culiacán, Mazatlán, and Navolato within the next 60–90 days, reducing the Chapitos to a guerrilla-style insurgency in rural areas. (Confidence: 60–70% / Likely)

*Basis:* The Mayiza's tactical superiority, the defection wave, and the collapse of Chapitos morale as indicated by the 60% decrease in social-media activity. The Chapitos' numerical advantage (2,000 lookouts) has been neutralized by the Mayiza's methodical intelligence-gathering. However, the Chapitos retain financial resources and could mount a counter-offensive if they reorganize.

**Judgment 2:** The Mayiza will fail to replicate El Mayo's state-capture network within the next 12 months, leaving them vulnerable to federal law enforcement and US extradition efforts. (Confidence: 70–80% / Highly Likely)

*Basis:* El Mayo's network was built over 40 years and was personal—based on bribes, favors, and family ties. His son, Ismael Zambada Sicairos, lacks the seniority and relationships to inherit this network. The LA Times (A2) reporting on El Mayo's plea deal confirms the depth of his corruption network, but also its personal nature. Without this protection, the Mayiza will be more vulnerable to the Mexican military and US DEA operations.

**Judgment 3:** The conflict will result in a fragmented Sinaloa Cartel, with the Mayiza controlling the core territory but unable to restore the CDS's former monopoly on drug trafficking in Sinaloa. (Confidence: 50–60% / Roughly Even Chance)

*Basis:* The Chapitos, though weakened, retain international connections (particularly with the CJNG in Jalisco) and financial reserves. They may survive as a smaller, more violent faction. The Mayiza's inability to capture the Chapitos' financial networks means the conflict could become a protracted, low-intensity war rather than a decisive victory. The defection wave could accelerate this fragmentation if more Chapitos operatives join the Mayiza, but it could also lead to the emergence of a third faction.
