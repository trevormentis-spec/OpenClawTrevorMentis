=== THE CRISIS ===

Keir Starmer's position has collapsed faster than any post-war Labour Prime Minister with a fresh majority. He took office in July 2024 with around 160 seats of cushion and a public mood that was less enthusiastic for Labour than exhausted with the Conservatives. Twenty-two months later, his net favourability sits at minus 46 (YouGov, May 2026), approval at 18 percent, and his own voters are split roughly down the middle on whether he should stay. That is the profile of a leader whose coalition has dissolved rather than frayed.

The proximate trigger is the May 2026 local elections, which on current reporting look like the worst local performance for a governing party since John Major in 1995. Losses at that scale do two things inside the Parliamentary Labour Party. They convince marginal-seat Members of Parliament that they personally will lose their jobs if nothing changes, and they give cover to ambitious cabinet ministers who have until now hedged. The BBC's reporting of a "coordinated attempt to get him to set a timetable for his departure" is the tell. That language describes an organised effort by named people, not backbench grumbling. Wikipedia's standing up of a dedicated "2026 Labour Party leadership crisis" page is a soft indicator that the crisis is being treated as a discrete historical event already.

The underlying causes are familiar: Reform UK is eating Labour's right flank in working-class seats while the Liberal Democrats and Greens nibble the left, Rachel Reeves' fiscal posture has alienated the activist base, and Starmer himself has never built a personal political brand independent of "not being a Conservative." When that contrast faded, so did he.

=== THE TIMETABLE ===

The betting markets are pricing a July-September 2026 exit at roughly 60 percent implied probability, with 2027-or-later at 31 percent. I think the market is roughly right on direction but slightly overconfident on the summer window. Here is why.

The mechanics of a Labour leadership change require either (a) Starmer resigning and triggering a contest, or (b) 20 percent of the Parliamentary Labour Party nominating an alternative, which under current rules is a high bar and historically untested in government. The path of least resistance is a managed exit: Starmer agrees in principle to go, a timetable is set, and a contest runs over the summer recess into conference season in late September.

Key dates that structure the decision:

The Makerfield by-election in June 2026 is the single most important event on the calendar. If Andy Burnham wins and re-enters Parliament, he becomes eligible to stand for leader and the pressure on Starmer becomes close to unmanageable. If Burnham loses or underperforms, the timetable slips right.

Parliamentary summer recess typically runs from late July to early September. Leadership manoeuvring traditionally accelerates in this window because Members of Parliament are in their constituencies hearing from angry activists.

Labour Party Conference is in late September. A new leader crowned at conference is the cleanest narrative outcome and the one party managers will push for.

My read: the most likely exit announcement comes between mid-June and late July, with a contest running August-September and a new leader in place by or at conference. Probability that Starmer is no longer Prime Minister by 30 September 2026: roughly 55-65 percent (probable).

=== THE CONTENDERS ===

Andy Burnham. The Manchester Mayor is the bookmakers' favourite at 10/11 and has 42 percent member support in the Compass survey, which is a commanding lead among the selectorate that actually picks Labour leaders. His strengths: a distinct political identity, northern English working-class authenticity that directly addresses the Reform problem, and a track record of executive delivery. His liabilities: he is not currently a Member of Parliament, he has lost two previous leadership contests (2010, 2015), and his relationship with the Parliamentary Labour Party is cooler than his relationship with members. The Makerfield by-election is the linchpin of his entire strategy. Politico's "even insiders don't know what Burnham has planned" is either genuine or, more likely, deliberate ambiguity.

Angela Rayner. Deputy Prime Minister, priced at 3.65 on Betfair (roughly 27 percent implied). Her path is the institutional one: if Starmer resigns, she becomes acting leader under Labour rules and runs with the advantages of incumbency. Her weaknesses are that she is less popular with members than Burnham and is tarnished by association with the government she would be inheriting. She is the candidate of continuity-with-a-different-face, which may or may not be what a battered party wants.

Wes Streeting. Health Secretary, 7.87 on Betfair, 11 percent member support. The candidate of the soft-Blairite right of the party. He has a parliamentary base and ministerial experience but the membership numbers tell you he cannot win a one-member-one-vote contest unless the field fractures. His likely role is kingmaker or stalking horse.

Ed Miliband. 11/1 is a courtesy price. He is not coming back.

Rachel Reeves. Absence from betting markets is correct. As Chancellor she is co-owner of the economic record that is sinking the government. She has no plausible path.

Dark horses to note: Lisa Nandy, Yvette Cooper, Shabana Mahmood. None currently positioned, but a long contest can produce surprises.

=== THE MOST LIKELY OUTCOME ===

Central forecast: Starmer announces a departure timetable between June and August 2026, a leadership contest runs through the summer, and Andy Burnham becomes Prime Minister at or shortly after Labour Conference in late September 2026, conditional on winning Makerfield.

I assess this as the modal outcome but not a majority outcome. Breaking it down:

Probability Starmer is no longer Prime Minister by end of September 2026: 55-65 percent (probable).

Conditional on Starmer leaving by September 2026, probability Burnham succeeds him: roughly 45-50 percent. Conditional probability Rayner succeeds him: roughly 30-35 percent. Streeting or other: 15-20 percent.

Unconditional probability Burnham is Prime Minister by end of September 2026: roughly 25-30 percent (roughly even chance, leaning against).

Unconditional probability Starmer is still Prime Minister at end of September 2026: roughly 35-45 percent (roughly even chance).

The single biggest uncertainty is whether Starmer fights. He has shown stubbornness before, and a sitting Prime Minister with a 160-seat majority has real institutional resources to resist. Eurasia Group's 25 percent estimate for orderly transition and 35 percent for forced contest by September aggregate to 60 percent, which aligns with the betting markets and with my central view.

=== SCENARIOS ===

Scenario one: Managed transition to Burnham. Probability roughly 30 percent (roughly even chance, leaning against). Burnham wins Makerfield in June, Starmer reads the runes and announces in July he will not lead Labour into the next election, a contest runs August-September, Burnham wins decisively on member votes, takes office at conference. The cleanest version of events for Labour.

Scenario two: Starmer hangs on through 2026. Probability roughly 30 percent (roughly even chance, leaning against). Starmer refuses to set a timetable, the Parliamentary Labour Party cannot organise a formal challenge under existing rules, cabinet ministers calculate that moving openly is too risky, and the crisis drags into 2027. He is weakened but in office. This is the "2027 or later" market scenario.

Scenario three: Forced contest with Rayner victorious. Probability roughly 15-20 percent (unlikely). Starmer is pushed out faster than he wants, possibly via mass cabinet resignations. Burnham is not yet in Parliament or has lost Makerfield. Rayner inherits as acting leader and consolidates with parliamentary backing before members can coalesce around an alternative.

Scenario four: Chaotic contest with surprise winner. Probability roughly 10-15 percent (unlikely). Burnham stumbles, Rayner is damaged by association with the government, Streeting or a dark horse like Mahmood or Cooper emerges from a fragmented field. Outcome unstable.

Residual probability for genuinely unexpected outcomes (Starmer dies or resigns for personal reasons, snap election, national security crisis reshuffles politics): roughly 5 percent.

=== WHAT TO WATCH ===

First, the Makerfield by-election result in June 2026. A Burnham win above 50 percent vote share confirms scenario one. A narrow win or loss reshuffles everything toward scenarios two and three.

Second, cabinet resignation patterns through June and July. Watch specifically for Rayner, Streeting, or any Secretary of State resigning on principle rather than being sacked. One such resignation is a tremor; two is an avalanche.

Third, the 1922-equivalent on the Labour side: signals from the Parliamentary Labour Party chair and from senior backbenchers about whether nomination thresholds for a challenge can realistically be met. Public letters from named Members of Parliament are the leading indicator.

Fourth, Starmer's own public language. If he starts talking about "the next phase" or "renewal" without committing to lead Labour into the next election, he is preparing the ground for exit. If he repeats commitments to serve a full term, he is digging in.

Fifth, Rachel Reeves' position. If Reeves is moved or resigns before Starmer, it suggests Starmer is trying to save himself by sacrificing his Chancellor, which historically buys a Prime Minister months but not years. If Reeves stays and Starmer goes, the new leader inherits the fiscal problem too.
