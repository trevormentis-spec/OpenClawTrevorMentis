═══ EXECUTIVE SUMMARY ═══
This week’s signal comes from the ground: a new FCC filing by Denali 20020, LLC—almost certainly Amazon’s Kuiper shell company—marks tangible movement on U.S. gateway licensing. In orbit, Starlink’s seven upcoming launches across three days (including two already in countdown) underscore relentless capacity expansion, adding stress to a ground network already burdened with a 247:1 satellite-to-gateway ratio. Market fundamentals hold: Starlink at 10M+ subs and $11.8B revenue anchors the LEO GSaaS segment, while the broader ground station market’s projected $82.7B by 2030 reminds us that non-LEO infrastructure still dominates the purse.

What changed: Kuiper’s licensing footprint materialized after months of silence, while the launch manifest confirms Starlink’s tempo and hints at potential Chinese LEO broadband activity via two Long March unknowns. What to watch: whether Denali filings accelerate into a wave of Kuiper gateway construction, and if Starlink responds with its own FCC gateways (curiously absent this cycle). Also, monitor those Chinese launches—if a Guowang build-out emerges, global ground station demand diversifies overnight.

═══ FCC LICENSES ═══
The 100-record round is dominated by legacy satellite operators (Intelsat, SES, Inmarsat) and backhaul providers (MCI, Sprint, Cogent Fiber, AT&T)—standard renewals and minor amendments. The lone exception: Denali 20020, LLC. This entity, sharing the Kuiper-affiliated “Denali” branding and a Washington, D.C. address, indicates Amazon is actively licensing U.S. gateway earth stations, likely for customer-facing Ka-band links. No SpaceX-associated filer (e.g., SpaceX Services or Starlink Services) appeared, suggesting Starlink’s ground licensing is either in a lull or handled under a radar-subtle shell. Calian Pacific Teleports and GOONHILLY INC. filings are routine for multi-orbit teleports. The Denali entry is the wedge—if it heralds a stream of Kuiper gateway applications, the ground segment landscape could shift from Starlink’s near-monopoly to a bimodal build-out by late 2026. For now, quantity is low, but the signal is clear: Kuiper’s ground program is no longer just paper.

═══ CONSTELLATION SIGNALS ═══
Starlink continues to muzzle-load the launch schedule. With 7 dedicated missions (three as next-to-fly, including Group 17-47 already go for June 3) out of 357 upcoming launches worldwide, the cadence is roughly one every two days. Each mission delivers ~21 V2 mini satellites, adding 147 sats to a fleet already at 9,400+. The ground network registers 38 Starlink sites, heavily concentrated in North America (26). That yields an average of 247 satellites per gateway—a ratio that strains contact scheduling and elevates latency jitter without more site density. I expect Starlink to file for additional gateways soon, possibly using a camouflage LLC to avoid the spotlight.

No Kuiper launches yet; first Atlas V Kuiper mission likely hold for a summer milestone. The two Chinese Long March launches (June 4 and 5) with unknown payloads are wildcards. If either carries LEO broadband test satellites (Guowang or Hongyan), it would validate China’s intent to compete in global ground infrastructure, forcing operators like KSAT, Leaf Space, and ATLAS to plan for a third constellation’s ground needs. For now, treat them as unconfirmed.
