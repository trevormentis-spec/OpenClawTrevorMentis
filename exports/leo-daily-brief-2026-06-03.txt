═══ EXECUTIVE SUMMARY ═══

Nothing moved the needle in ground station licenses this cycle, but the contrast between orbital deployment and terrestrial infrastructure is sharpening into a warning. Three Starlink launches in the next five days will push the constellation past 9,400 active satellites; our site register credits SpaceX with only 38 known gateway sites globally. Even allowing for optical inter-satellite links reducing the need for contiguous ground coverage, the ratio implies a future capacity choke point as the subscriber base—already north of 10 million—grows toward the 14-million 2030 estimate. The silent registries (FCC, ITU) and a single soft jobs hit suggest the industry is either executing quietly through legacy instruments or has yet to commit capital to the build-out that a $5.33 billion LEO GSaaS market demands.

What to watch: any sudden flurry of new filings or construction imagery—especially from Kuiper, which holds only five known sites, and from China’s opaque Long March payloads. If the Chinese constellations are real, they will require mid-latitude gateways outside their territory, and the current ITU vacuum cannot last. The bottom line is that ground segment capacity risk is underpriced; we should forecast a wave of site acquisitions or multi-operator partnerships by late 2026 and advise clients to lock in spectrum and real estate now.

═══ FCC LICENSES ═══

The FCC feed is a catalog of incumbents—MCI, Intelsat, Inmarsat, AT&T, SES Americom, and their corporate successors. None of the LEO-native operators (SpaceX, Amazon, Telesat, OneWeb) appear as new filers, nor do the cloud providers dipping into GSaaS. This likely reflects two things: first, Starlink's gateway footprint in the US was largely licensed by 2024 under experimental or special temporary authorizations now converted to permanent, without newsworthy changes; second, many international players avoid the FCC entirely by routing traffic through allied jurisdictions. Still, 100 active records with no fresh LEO signatures means the US regulatory front is quiet. If Kuiper intends to meet its FCC deployment milestones, it will need to surface soon—silence here is notable.

═══ CONSTELLATION SIGNALS ═══

Starlink is launching at a run rate that adds roughly 120-180 satellites per week. The upcoming sequence—Group 17-47 today, 10-43 tomorrow, 17-43 on June 7—will fill shells optimized for high-inclination and mid-latitude coverage. With 9,400+ spacecraft already radiating, the ground network must handle terabits of aggregated traffic daily. Our register counts 38 Starlink-associated sites, concentrated in North America (26) and Europe (18 by region, partially overlapping with operator counts). That footprint can cover the continental US and parts of Europe well, but it leaves Asia (8 sites), Oceania (7), and South America (6) thin, and Africa (5) almost negligible. If Starlink is relying on laser crosslinks to serve underserved areas without local gateways, then latency and trunk backhaul become dependent on the health of a few key sites, increasing systemic risk in the event of a cable cut or political disruption. Meanwhile, OneWeb (10 sites) and Telesat (3) remain boutique. The two Chinese Long March launches are cryptically labelled "Unknown Payload." They are consistent with Guowang or Hongyan precursor missions, yet no associated ITU ground station filings appear, meaning either they use Russian or allied territory as gateways or they have not yet begun coordinating international spectrum—a tactic that will force a confrontation eventually.
